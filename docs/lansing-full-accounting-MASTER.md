# The Full Accounting
## Community Benefit, Socialized Cost, and a Plan Forward
### A Polycentric Governance Framework for Lansing

*This document is a companion to the Lansing Merged Timeline. It attempts an honest accounting of what each major development in Lansing's history actually delivered — and what it actually cost — and draws from that accounting a specific, actionable plan for what comes next.*

---

## Why This Accounting Matters

The standard civic narrative about Lansing's industrial history gives credit without cost. Ransom Olds is a visionary. The auto industry built the middle class. The riverfront is revitalized. The housing commission is doing its best with limited resources.

None of that is entirely wrong. But none of it is the whole picture either.

The full accounting requires asking three questions for every development:

1. **Who received the benefit?** (specifically, not generally)
2. **Who bore the cost?** (specifically, not generally)
3. **Was that distribution the result of a decision, or was it inevitable?**

The answer to the third question, consistently, is: it was a decision. And the decisions were made in a governance structure that concentrated power at the center and left the most affected communities with the least ability to shape the outcome.

That is what polycentric governance is designed to change. Every case below shares a structural feature: **a decision was made by one center of power, without a binding mechanism for the affected community to contest it, and the cost landed on people with no institutional recourse.** That is not a coincidence — it is what monocentric governance produces.

## Case 0: The Land Before the Cases (1819–1862)

*Every case below assumes a governed city already exists — a council, a housing commission, a utility board. This section covers what came before any of that: the land itself, how it changed hands, and what was built on the proceeds. It's placed as Case 0 rather than folded into the numbered sequence because it's a different category of harm than Cases 1–12 — not a governance failure inside an existing institution, but the founding transaction that made "Lansing" a place with institutions at all.*

### 0a: The Treaty of Saginaw and the Treaty of Washington (1819, 1836)

#### What was received:
The land that became Lansing, East Lansing, and the surrounding region — including what is now Michigan State University's campus — entered U.S. federal control through the **1819 Treaty of Saginaw** and two **1836 treaties** (the Treaty of Washington among them). This land became the basis for state formation, township organization, and eventually every institution documented in this file.

#### What it actually cost:
This land was the ancestral, traditional, and contemporary territory of the **Anishinaabeg — the Three Fires Confederacy of Ojibwe, Odawa, and Potawatomi peoples**, along with the Saginaw Band of Chippewa specifically named in the 1819 treaty. Michigan State University's own current land acknowledgment materials state plainly that the treaty was negotiated "under coercive or violent circumstances," and that "settler and Indigenous signatories understood the terms of the treaties in starkly different terms." An Anishinaabe "Indian Encampment" is documented on university archive maps as still present just south of the Red Cedar River when the first classes were held in 1857 — meaning displacement was not a distant historical abstraction to the institution's founders, it was visibly ongoing on the same ground.

#### The accounting gap:
There is no dollar figure that honestly captures this cost, and this document will not manufacture one. What can be stated honestly: every subsequent case in this accounting — every highway, every housing commission decision, every hospital merger — takes place on land whose original transfer involved no binding consent from the people who held it, and no seat at the table of the kind this entire framework argues every later decision should have had. This is the pattern's first documented instance in this specific place, not a metaphor for it.

**Sources:** Michigan State University American Indian and Indigenous Studies Program (land acknowledgment materials, aiis.msu.edu); MSU Native American Institute; MSU brand/history office historical materials.

### 0b: Michigan Agricultural College and the Morrill Act (1855–1862)

#### What the community genuinely received:
In 1855, Governor Kinsley Bingham signed the bill creating the **Agricultural College of the State of Michigan** — the institution that became Michigan State University — the first institution in the United States to teach scientific agriculture, and explicitly designed to extend higher education "regardless of social class" at a time when college was almost exclusively a privilege of the wealthy. It became the direct model for the **1862 Morrill Act**, the federal law that created the entire American land-grant university system — more than 100 institutions nationally trace their funding to this model.

#### What it actually cost:
The Morrill Act funded these colleges by granting states federal public land — or "land scrip" where a state lacked sufficient public land within its own borders — to be sold, with proceeds funding the colleges. Nationally, this system distributed **land scrip drawn from roughly 10.7 million acres taken from 245 tribal nations**, per Michigan State University's own American Indian and Indigenous Studies program. Michigan's specific land grant, tied to Michigan Agricultural College, drew from parcels ceded in the 1819 Treaty of Saginaw and the 1836 treaties described in Case 0a.

**A formula-based estimate, not a confirmed transaction record:** the Morrill Act allocated each state 30,000 acres per congressional seat (House and Senate combined), valued in the statute at $1.25 per acre. Michigan held roughly 8 congressional seats around 1862 (2 senators, approximately 6 representatives following the 1860 apportionment). Applying the Act's own formula: 8 seats × 30,000 acres = **240,000 acres**, valued at $1.25/acre = **approximately $300,000 in 1862 dollars**. This is a calculation from the statute's stated terms, not a verified record of Michigan's actual specific land sales, which may have differed in practice (some states sold scrip in other states' territory at varying prices). It should be treated as an illustrative order-of-magnitude figure pending direct archival confirmation, not a precise historical fact.

**In 2026 dollars, using the extended historical price index described in the Methodology section below, $300,000 in 1862 is approximately $9.9 million.** That is the estimated value of the land transferred — without the consent of the nations it was taken from — to fund the founding of what is now a multi-billion-dollar public research university two miles from the State Capitol.

#### The accounting gap:
Michigan Agricultural College's founding mission — education "regardless of social class" — was genuinely realized for the settlers and their descendants who attended it, including, generations later, people directly connected to this project (MSU is where Dr. Deyanira Nevarez Martinez teaches, and where Raven Norris studied genetics before going to work at Cedars-Sinai). But the founding capital for that mission came from land whose original inhabitants received none of that opportunity in return. This is the same benefit/cost asymmetry as every case that follows — the people who received the university's opportunity and the people who paid for its founding land were not the same people, and no version of consent, compensation, or seat-at-the-table existed to reconcile that at the time.

**Sources:** MSU American Indian and Indigenous Studies Program (aiis.msu.edu); MSU Native American Institute (nai.msu.edu); National Archives (Morrill Act, Public Law 37-108, July 2, 1862); Encyclopedia.com (Morrill Act statutory text, $1.25/acre valuation); MSU Today, "Land-Grant Roots" (July 2, 2018); MSU CANR Tribal Extension program.

### 0c: Biddle City (1835)

#### What was promised:
New York land speculators sold lots in "Biddle City" — described to buyers as a promising, established settlement in Michigan Territory. Buyers purchased sight unseen.

#### What it actually was:
When buyers arrived, they found frozen wilderness. Nothing had been built. The land — recently made available for sale following the treaty cessions described in Case 0a — was sold to people with no way to verify the claim before money changed hands, by people who were not present and bore none of the consequences of the fraud.

#### The accounting gap:
This is not a governance failure — there was no governance structure yet to fail. It is the clearest possible statement of the pattern in its rawest form, occurring within a single decade of the land itself being transferred out of Anishinaabe control: **a decision made by people who bear none of its consequences, imposed on people with no way to contest it before harm occurs.** Every case in this document that follows is a more institutionally sophisticated version of the same basic move. No reliable dollar figure for the specific fraud has been located; this is included for its structural significance to the pattern, not its financial scale.

**Sources:** General Michigan territorial history; this account should be treated as a documented historical anecdote pending a specific primary-source citation, which has not yet been located in this project's research — flagged as an open research thread below.

## Cost-Benefit and Inflation Adjustment Methodology

Every dollar figure in this document is sourced at its original, nominal value — the number as reported at the time. Nominal values are never silently replaced with inflation-adjusted ones, because the nominal figure is what any citation actually verifies. Where an inflation-adjusted comparison adds real value — primarily in the Cumulative Ledger below, where figures spanning 1862 to 2026 would otherwise be compared as if a 1990 dollar and a 2026 dollar were the same thing — both the nominal and the adjusted figure are shown, clearly labeled.

**Method:** For years 1913 forward, adjustments use the official U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index (CPI-U), via cited inflation-calculator sources reflecting BLS data as of mid-2026. For 1862 — before the CPI existed — the adjustment uses an extended historical price index (as published by in2013dollars.com, sourced ultimately to pre-CPI historical price research blended with BLS data from 1913 forward) and is explicitly flagged as a rougher, less precise estimate than any post-1913 figure in this document.

**Multipliers used (to 2026), each independently sourced:**
- 1862 → 2026: **×33** (extended historical index; flagged as an estimate, not official CPI)
- 1990 → 2026: **×2.37** (official CPI-U)
- 2018 → 2026: **×1.33** (official CPI-U)
- 2020 → 2026: **×1.27** (calculated by compounding published annual CPI-U changes 2021–2026; treat as a good-faith rounded estimate rather than a precise BLS lookup)
- 2021 → 2026: **×1.22** (same method as above)
- 2023 → 2026: **×1.08** (same method as above)
- 2024 → 2026: **×1.05** (same method as above)
- 2025 → 2026: **×1.03** (same method as above)

**What this pass covers, and what it doesn't yet:** this methodology has been applied to the Cumulative Ledger table below, which is the document's single consolidated cost summary and the place a mismatched-year comparison would do the most damage to the argument. It has **not** yet been applied line-by-line to every dollar figure inside the prose of Cases 1 through 12 — that would be a substantial additional editing pass across an already-large document. Flagged here as a clear next step rather than silently left incomplete.

## Cost-Benefit Ratio Methodology (for future case-by-case application)

For any case where a benefit-cost ratio is attempted, four fields are used rather than a single number:
1. **Documented Public Cost ($)** — sourced only, nominal value.
2. **Documented Public Benefit ($)** — split into *Projected* (what was claimed at approval) and *Realized* (what's actually been verified as delivered).
3. **Benefit-Cost Ratio** — computed only when both sides are genuinely quantified in comparable terms. Marked **N/A** when one side isn't monetizable — which will be most cases in this document — rather than forcing a number. An N/A is itself a finding: it usually means a project was approved without anyone pricing its benefit against its cost in the first place.
4. **Distributional Match** — Same / Different / Mixed: did the population receiving the benefit overlap with the population bearing the cost? This keeps a ratio from being misread as "was this a good deal" instead of "a good deal, and for whom."

This framework has not yet been applied case-by-case across the document; it's recorded here as the agreed method for when that pass happens.

## The Case Template

*As of August 2026, lansing.love's published case studies use a richer analytical template than this document's original five-part format. That site template is now the standard — this document is being migrated to match it, case by case. Every case going forward should use this structure. This is a template migration in progress: Cases 7 and 8 below have been rebuilt in full; the remaining cases still use the original five-part format pending the same treatment.*

**1. Header stats** — a handful of the case's sharpest hard numbers, pulled out as standalone figures before any prose (e.g., "51 families evicted," "2.7:1 demolitions per unit created"). These are the numbers a reader remembers.

**2. Summary** — two to four sentences stating the pattern plainly: what the institution was supposed to do, what it actually did instead.

**3. The Seven ICA Cooperative Principles — applied.** The International Cooperative Alliance's seven principles, used here as an accountability framework rather than a cooperative-membership checklist:
   1. Voluntary and Open Membership
   2. Democratic Member Control
   3. Member Economic Participation
   4. Autonomy and Independence
   5. Education, Training, and Information
   6. Cooperation Among Cooperatives
   7. Concern for Community

   Each principle gets a short paragraph naming how this case violates or upholds it, plus one specific evidentiary citation.

**4. The Five Ownership Questions** — a table, Before/After/Assessment, answering: Who owns it? Who has power? Who benefits? Who does the work? Who makes the rules? Assessment is EXTRACTIVE, NON-EXTRACTIVE, or MIXED for each row.

**5. The Four Bottom Lines** — People, Planet, Prosperity, Purpose. Each gets a one-word rating (negative / positive / mixed / insufficient) and a short justification.

**6. What the community genuinely received** — the real, honest benefits, stated without hedging. Every case gets this, including the worst ones — pretending there's no real benefit anywhere is as dishonest as pretending there's no real cost.

**7. The pattern / What it actually cost** — the specific mechanism of extraction, broken into named sub-components where useful (e.g., "tax credit syndication," "asset disposition," "management contracting" as separate line items rather than one paragraph).

**8. Named conflicts of interest** — every person with a documented conflict, named individually with their specific overlapping roles and specific votes or actions, not summarized as a group.

**9. Explore the data / Tools** — links to any interactive tool (built by this project or referenced from it) that lets a reader explore the underlying data directly, not just read the case's conclusions about it.

**10. Recommendations** — concrete, specific accountability actions, phrased as things that could actually be done, not vague aspirations.

**11. Sources** — every source cited, including cross-references to other parts of this project (other cases, the timeline, this document itself) where relevant.

**12. Pattern Language cross-references** — every pattern from `polycentric-pattern-language.md` that this case is a documented instance of, by number and name, linking the narrative accounting to the reusable governance framework.

### Case 1: Oldsmobile / GM Industrial Complex (1901–2005)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **104 years of manufacturing employment** — at its peak, the Lansing Car Assembly was ranked the #1 most productive automobile plant in North America by The Harbour Report (2002). At closure, GM's combined Lansing operations employed approximately 3,900 people in the two remaining plants.
- **Wages above the regional average** — UAW-negotiated wages provided working-class and Black families pathways to homeownership and economic stability that were otherwise structurally closed to them under FHA redlining.
- **Supplier ecosystem** — GM's presence anchored a regional manufacturing supply chain. The R.E. Olds Museum documents over 40 Lansing-area companies that grew directly from Olds-related operations, including Atlas Drop Forge, Motor Wheel Corp., and Fisher Body.
- **Philanthropic investment** — R.E. Olds personally funded the replacement engineering school at MSU after it burned, likely saving MSU's independence from University of Michigan annexation. The R.E. Olds Foundation continues small-grant community philanthropy.
- **Tax base** — For over a century, GM's Lansing operations contributed substantially to the city and county tax base, funding schools, roads, and public services.

#### What it actually cost:
- **Neighborhood displacement via I-496** — The community that built itself around GM jobs was demolished by the highway that served GM's workforce commute. 840+ homes, 600+ families, nearly all Black. The benefit (jobs, wages) and the cost (neighborhood destruction) were distributed to different populations.
- **PFAS and industrial contamination** — PFOS/PFOA from chrome plating at Plant 3. 1,4-dioxane contamination. Adams Plating PFAS from adjacent electroplating 1964–2010. Remediation cost: **$40M+ in public money committed, costs still accumulating.**
- **Coal plant health burden** — 97 years of coal combustion adjacent to the same neighborhood that bore the displacement cost. No documented health impact study was conducted.
- **GM bankruptcy liability shed** — When GM left, the cleanup bill went to RACER Trust and eventually to the public. The private wealth extracted over 104 years did not stay to cover the environmental liability it created.
- **No community benefit agreement** — When Mayor Hollister's Blue Ribbon Committee persuaded GM to stay in the late 1990s and build three new plants, the city negotiated retention but not binding community benefits, environmental cleanup bonds, or transition funds. The city gave; GM stayed on its own terms.

#### The accounting gap:
The wages were real. The tax base was real. The supply chain was real. But they accrued primarily to workers (many of whom were later displaced or laid off), to shareholders (who were never Lansing residents in any meaningful proportion), and to the city's general fund. The contamination, the displacement, and the cleanup bill accrued primarily to the Black community adjacent to the plant, and then to all Lansing taxpayers. **The beneficiaries and the cost-bearers were not the same people.**

RACER Trust context: the three Lansing-area GM sites (Plants 2, 3, and 6) total over 234 acres. A developer has held them under contract since 2016 and still cannot finalize a purchase until cleanup is complete. Michigan committed an additional $19 million in 2024 for the Fisher Body site alone — costs still climbing.

### Case 2: Eckert Station / BWL Coal Operations (1922–2020)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **97 years of reliable municipal electricity** — at 351 megawatts, Eckert powered downtown Lansing and the GM Grand River plant. Municipal ownership meant rates stayed lower than private utility alternatives.
- **BWL as a publicly owned utility** — unlike investor-owned utilities, BWL's surplus flows back to city operations. The city receives an annual transfer from BWL that funds public services. This is a genuine structural benefit of public ownership.
- **Steam district heating** — BWL's steam system heated downtown buildings for decades, reducing individual building boiler costs.

#### What it actually cost:
- **Coal combustion health burden** — 97 years of particulate matter, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and mercury emissions adjacent to a residential neighborhood. No epidemiological study of the cancer or respiratory disease burden in the adjacent community has been published for this site.
- **Erickson coal ash contamination** — At the Delta Township plant (opened 1973), pollutants leaked from coal ash ponds. Federal groundwater monitoring results came in three years late under the 2015 federal rule. $21M+ in public cleanup costs documented. BWL asked for delays even after documenting the contamination. BWL announced all ash removed to a regulated landfill by June 2024.
- **Red Cedar and Grand River pollution** — Coal ash leachate contributes to watershed contamination. The Red Cedar has had total-body-contact advisories approximately 70% of the time since 2000.
- **Decommissioning cost** — Ratepayers fund decommissioning. The Eckert site transition to a substation and the Erickson ash cleanup are public costs following private-equivalent profit extraction during the operating years.

#### The accounting gap:
BWL's public ownership is a genuine structural advantage — ratepayers benefit from the nonprofit model in ways that investor-owned utility customers do not. But public ownership of the *institution* did not translate into accountability for the *externalities*. The coal ash contamination, the air quality burden, and the groundwater risk were all externalized onto adjacent communities and future ratepayers, even under public ownership. **Public ownership is necessary but not sufficient. Governance accountability to affected communities — not just to appointed commissioners — is what was missing.**

### Case 3: I-496 / Highway Infrastructure (1963–1970)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **Regional connectivity** — I-496 connects I-96 to downtown Lansing and provided a genuine transportation improvement for commuters across the region.
- **Property value increases** in areas adjacent to improved access (though not in the neighborhood demolished to build it).
- **Reduced commute times** for suburban residents entering downtown.

#### What it actually cost:
- **840+ homes and businesses demolished** via eminent domain.
- **600+ families displaced**, nearly all Black, into a redlined housing market.
- **Neighborhood wealth destruction** — families who had paid off mortgages were forced to restart. Generational wealth compounded across decades.
- **Community fabric destroyed** — churches, social clubs, the only rhythm and blues record store in Lansing. The social capital of a functioning neighborhood cannot be priced, but its destruction has documented consequences.
- **Ongoing public maintenance cost** — The 2020 Pave the Way reconstruction cost $42M. The 2024–2025 US-127/I-496 corridor project is a $205M investment. The proposed I-496 cap study (2025 grant) is $1M, with construction costs entirely unfunded.
- **Health and environmental costs** — The highway severed the neighborhood and concentrated traffic pollution over the remaining hemmed-in residents for 60+ years.

#### The accounting gap:
The highway benefited primarily suburban commuters and regional freight movement — populations that were not displaced to build it. The cost was borne primarily by the Black community whose neighborhood was in the path. The 2025 cap study grant is a public acknowledgment that the original decision caused harm requiring remediation — but the $1M study doesn't cover the construction cost of the cap, which will again be a public expense. **The community that paid the cost in 1963 is not the community receiving the remediation investment in 2025.**

**Governance note:** the decision traces to the city's 1958 Comprehensive Master Plan, made by state highway planners with no binding council, veto, or right of first refusal for the neighborhood in its path. Community leaders at the time lobbied for relocation assistance *instead of* fighting the highway, because the monocentric structure left them no other lever. The highway was named the R.E. Olds Freeway — after the same industrialist whose plant drew the workforce that built the neighborhood it destroyed.

### Case 4: Frandor / Montgomery Drain (1954–2026)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **Retail access and jobs** — Frandor provided regional retail employment and consumer convenience for 70 years.
- **Tax base contribution** — Commercial property taxes from Frandor and surrounding development contributed to city and county revenues.

#### What it actually cost:
- **80% impervious surface** in the Montgomery Drain service area — 60 years of stormwater runoff directly into the Red Cedar.
- **80 documented pollutants** in the Red Cedar River — arsenic, petroleum, rubber, asbestos, acids.
- **Total-body-contact advisories 70% of the time** since 2000. The Red Cedar runs through a major university town. You cannot swim in it.
- **$50M public Montgomery Drain reconstruction** — paid by a city-wide drain tax and property owner assessments. Frandor's private owners contributed nothing beyond their proportional assessment.
- **Ongoing flooding** — as recently as April 2026, businesses near East Kalamazoo Street flooded for days after heavy rains.

#### The accounting gap:
Frandor's owners extracted 70 years of retail profit from an 80% impervious landscape with no stormwater accounting required. The public is now spending $50M to fix the watershed. This is the clearest case in Lansing's history of **private profit, public cleanup** — and it has a dollar figure attached to it.

**The legal lock-in — *Bolt v. City of Lansing* (1998):** *Bolt v. City of Lansing*, 459 Mich. 152 (1998), ruled Lansing's stormwater service charge — intended to fund the $176M combined sewer separation required by the Clean Water Act — an unconstitutional tax requiring voter approval, and established a three-part test for valid user fees (regulatory purpose, proportionate to costs, voluntary). The Lansing Regional Chamber of Commerce filed the amicus brief for the challenging taxpayer, against the city's ability to fund public stormwater infrastructure — the same Chamber whose members' development patterns created the runoff problem in the first place. The ruling remains controlling Michigan law today. The arc in full: impervious surface created → runoff overwhelmed sewers → sewage into the Grand River → city tried to charge property owners → Chamber challenged → Supreme Court killed the fee → $50M public remediation follows.

### Case 5: Michigan Accident Fund / AF Group / Ottawa Street Station (1912–2026)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **78 years of public workers' compensation** — the Michigan Accident Fund provided accessible, publicly accountable workers' comp coverage from 1912 to 1990, as one of the nation's first state-run programs.
- **Downtown anchor employer** — AF Group became the largest private employer in downtown Lansing after occupying the Ottawa Street Station, providing a stabilizing employment presence in a post-industrial core.
- **Historic preservation** — the conversion of the Ottawa Street Station was a genuine architectural preservation achievement. The Art Deco landmark was saved.

#### What it actually cost:
- **$291M privatization** — a public institution built over 78 years was sold for $291M. That capital went to the state general fund one time, rather than continuing to generate public benefit indefinitely.
- **Workers' comp access** — privatization shifted the institution's primary accountability from policyholders and workers to shareholders. Premium structures and claims management changed accordingly.
- **2026 sale to Enstar/Sixth Street** — the institution is now a global reinsurance asset held by a Bermuda-based investment vehicle. The distance between the institution's original public mission and its current ownership is 114 years and several thousand miles.

#### The accounting gap:
The historic preservation benefit is real. The downtown anchor employer benefit is real. But both were achieved by taking a public institution built over 78 years and giving it to a succession of private owners — each further removed from accountability to Lansing residents. **The preservation of the building should not be confused with the preservation of the institution's public purpose.**

### Case 6: City Market / Lansing Shuffle / Rotary Park (1938–2026)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **81 years of public food access** — the WPA-built City Market served as a genuine food commons from 1938 to 2019. At its peak, you "couldn't even get to the tables."
- **Riverfront activation** — Rotary Park and the Lansing Shuffle have contributed to downtown foot traffic and riverfront use.
- **$4.2M private investment** in a building the city was not maintaining effectively.

#### What it actually cost:
- **Loss of food commons function** — the market's food access function is gone. The building is now a shuffleboard bar.
- **$953K sale at 2018 appraisal, no independent reappraisal** — the price offered was set eight years before the vote.
- **$2,000/month lease revenue at stake** — the parks department stood to lose $24,000/year in perpetuity in exchange for a one-time $953K payment, had the sale gone through.
- **Rotary Park beach unusable** — the park built around the river cannot be used for swimming due to E. coli. The amenity exists; the actual use is foreclosed by pollution.

**Update — August 4, 2026: voters rejected the sale, 53% to 47%.** The ballot question asked whether the city could negotiate the sale of the property to Detroit Rising Development, its current operator, at the $953,000 figure. Detroit Rising co-owner Jon Hartzell has said he intends to push to put the sale back on the ballot in a future election, framing the loss as a narrative problem to be solved through "relationship building" rather than a signal about the price or process. The Lansing Park Board had voted against recommending the sale go to the ballot at all; the Planning Commission voted in favor. City Council placed it on the ballot anyway, 6-1, with Ryan Kost as the lone dissent — he cited the outdated appraisal and warned that the buy-back clause would be financially unreachable for the city if it ever wanted the property returned.

#### The accounting gap:
The Lansing Shuffle is a functioning business that activates the riverfront. That is a genuine benefit. But it was achieved by converting a public food commons into a private entertainment venue, and the purchase option written into the 2021 lease means the conversion to private ownership was the plan from the beginning — not an outcome the public was asked to weigh in on until 2026, when they finally were, and said no. **The community received a bar where it had a market, was asked to ratify the sale of the land beneath it, and refused.** This is one of the only outright wins documented anywhere in this accounting — worth stating plainly rather than folding into the same tone as the losses. The open question is whether "no" holds: Hartzell's stated plan to bring it back to a future ballot means this case is not closed, only paused.

Rotary Park funding detail: Delta Dental ($250,000), Auto-Owners Insurance ($75,000), Gillespie Group ($30,000), and the Rotary Club of Lansing ($400,000 — lead donor and namesake), matched dollar-for-dollar by the Capital Region Community Foundation. The Foundation's explicit stated mission: *"to attract and retain a talented workforce for our region"* — a talent-retention strategy for the knowledge economy, not a public parks mission.

### Case 7: Lansing Housing Commission Dispositions (2020–2026)

*Corrected: previously titled "(2018-2026)." No specific 2018 event could be found anchoring that start date after direct research — what's actually documented is RAD conversion construction beginning around 2020 (South Washington Park, Mount Vernon Park) and the SK Investment Group sale process running Oct.-Dec. 2021 (proposals sought) through 2022 (HUD approval, closing). Jerry confirmed he isn't sure what 2018 was meant to reference either, and agreed 2020 is the more defensible start date.*

**Header stats:** 51 families evicted · 14 evicted in a single day · 25% eviction rate · 84 net units lost · 85+ children displaced mid-school-year · $17.7M total sale/grant proceeds · $357K cost per unit built vs. $87.6K sale price per unit · 113 eviction cases filed (51 resulted in completed eviction, marked CLOSED; the remainder closed without eviction) · 833 to 66 publicly owned units (92% reduction)

**Summary:** The Lansing Housing Commission's operations exemplify how housing privatization systematically extracts wealth from communities while claiming to serve them. The SK Investment Group transaction, tax credit syndication through Cinnaire, and federal grant accumulation represent a coordinated shift from maximizing affordable housing units to maximizing financial returns for private partners. LHC's operational model violates every ICA cooperative principle, and all five ownership-question dimensions show extractive characteristics.

#### The Seven ICA Cooperative Principles — applied

**1. Voluntary and Open Membership.** 51 families evicted — 14 in a single day — through court proceedings with no voluntary exit process. New residents are selected by private landlord criteria, not community need. *Evidence: MiCourt records show 51 eviction cases filed by SK Lansing, all marked CLOSED.*

**2. Democratic Member Control.** Residents had no vote in the decision to sell their homes. 40 families expressed interest in purchasing their units; only 8 qualified. No documented resident consultation occurred during the sale process. *Evidence: no resident representation existed on the LHC board during the sale.*

**3. Member Economic Participation.** Residents received zero share of $17.7M in combined sale and grant proceeds. Capital gains were captured entirely by LHC and transferred to a private entity. *Evidence: $17.7M in proceeds; zero resident benefit-sharing; LHC accumulated $30M+ including federal grants over the period.*

**4. Autonomy and Independence.** Community lost autonomous control over its own housing. The properties are now controlled by a Florida-based investment firm (SK Investment Group, reportedly 1,500+ units across multiple states) rather than any local or resident-controlled entity. A 20-year covenant creates continued dependency, not autonomy. *Evidence: SK Investment Group is based in South Florida; Lansing's properties are managed as one part of an external, multi-state investment portfolio.*

**5. Education, Training, and Information.** Residents were told "very few" would need to relocate. The actual eviction rate was 25%. Subsequent FOIA requests for records were blocked with $4,430–$807.60 in fees (figures vary by request), using what reporting describes as the wrong legal test for the request. *Evidence: promises of minimal displacement directly contradicted by 51 completed eviction filings.*

**6. Cooperation Among Cooperatives.** LHC partnered with extractive private capital rather than exploring a community land trust, resident cooperative, or community development corporation structure. ### Case 8: The Ingham County Land Bank (2005–2026)

**Header stats:** 2.7:1 demolitions-per-unit-created ratio · 472 parcels currently owned in Lansing · $976K demolition spending (2024) · $1.5M grant redirected to a post-selection LLC · $1 insider property acquisition · 0 documented competitive bids · 22–56 minute average board meeting length · 1 dissenting vote recorded across 5 years · 47:15 demolitions-vs.-completions in the 5-year record (roughly 800:297 over 15 years)

**Summary:** The Ingham County Land Bank was created to return vacant and tax-foreclosed property to productive use. Over 20 years, its operations have produced close to the opposite outcome: a 2.7-to-1 demolition-to-creation ratio, a 472-parcel landholding in a city with a severe housing shortage, and a procurement pipeline that consistently routes public funds to a small circle of preferred partners without competitive bidding.

#### The Seven ICA Cooperative Principles — applied

**1. Voluntary and Open Membership.** Residents have no meaningful choice when the Land Bank acquires tax-foreclosed property from their neighborhood or demolishes adjacent structures. *Evidence: the Land Bank has removed homeless encampments from its own vacant parcels with no alternative proposal documented, per board meeting notes.*

**2. Democratic Member Control.** Board meetings average 22–56 minutes with zero documented public comment across roughly 20 meetings reviewed (2020–2026). Only one dissenting vote was recorded across five years. *Evidence: board minutes 2020–2026.*

**3. Member Economic Participation.** Public funds ($976K in demolition grants, $1.5M+ in redirected grants) flow to preferred partners without competitive bidding, while the community receives demolitions and vacant lots rather than housing. *Evidence: the Pleasant Grove project redirected $1.5M in county grants to an LLC formed three days after the developer was selected, with no competitive bidding process documented.*

**4. Autonomy and Independence.** Every board member holds an overlapping role elsewhere in county or city government. *Evidence: Alan Fox (Chair) is the Ingham County Treasurer, controlling the tax-foreclosure pipeline that supplies Land Bank inventory. Rawley Van Fossen (Treasurer) is the City of Lansing's Director of Economic Development and Planning, issuing demolition permits on properties the Land Bank sells.*

**5. Education, Training, and Information.** No public dashboard shows parcel acquisitions, sales, prices, or buyer identities. *Evidence: in April 2025, the board authorized a preferred buyer (Cooney Homes) to purchase multiple parcels without specifying which properties.*

**6. Cooperation Among Cooperatives.** Public-funded parcels are directed to CAHP, Cooney Homes, and Allen Edwin rather than cooperative housing alternatives or community land trusts. *Evidence: CAHP receives below-market acquisitions (averaging $5,610) while paying the Land Bank quarterly rental-management fees — a circular arrangement, since Emma Henry runs both CAHP and the Lansing Housing Commission.*

**7. Concern for Community.** A 2.7-to-1 demolition ratio persists during a documented ~119,000-unit statewide housing deficit; 472 parcels sit idle while Lansing rents have risen roughly 25%. *Evidence: the 15-year record (2005–2020) shows approximately 800 demolitions against 297 units created.*

#### The Five Ownership Questions

| Question | Before | After | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns it? | Ingham County (public, tax-foreclosed) | Preferred private buyers — CAHP, Cooney Homes, Allen Edwin — without a competitive process | EXTRACTIVE |
| Who has power? | Board (nominally public) | The same board members acting in their other government roles — self-referential authority | EXTRACTIVE |
| Who benefits? | Community (the statutory purpose) | CAHP, preferred developers, and HOA self-management entities controlled by Land Bank staff | EXTRACTIVE |

#### The Four Bottom Lines

- **People — negative.** The 2.7:1 demolition ratio reduces housing supply during a documented crisis. 472 parcels sit idle while rents rise; no affordability restrictions apply to market-rate renovations.
- **Planet — mixed.** Demolition removes deteriorated structures but leaves vacant, unproductive lots with no documented green or energy-efficient redevelopment plan.
- **Prosperity — negative.** $1.5M+ in public funds flow to insider partners without competition; parcels are sold below market rate to preferred buyers.
- **Purpose — negative.** The statutory mission — returning property to productive use — is directly contradicted by a 20-year demolition-heavy record. The Land Bank functions as a demolition contractor with a preferred-partner resale pipeline, not a housing development institution.

#### What the community genuinely received:
- A mechanism for returning tax-foreclosed properties to some form of use, in principle preventing indefinite blight.
- Genuinely renovated homes in a small number of documented cases, including houses donated by Sparrow and renovated at zero acquisition cost.

#### The pattern — what it actually cost:
- **Approximately 47 demolitions against 15 housing-unit completions** in the documented five-year record, continuing a 15-year pattern of roughly 800 demolitions against 297 units created.
- **The Pleasant Grove pipeline** — developer Brent Forsberg was selected via a two-response RFP (September 2024); Holmes & Pleasant Grove LLC was formed June 3, 2025, three days after selection; $1.5M in county grants were redirected to the new entity, alongside $4,500/month in separate personal consulting payments (Oct.–Dec. 2025).
- **The $1 acquisition** — the Land Bank acquired a gas-damaged house from its own Garden Program Coordinator for $1 (December 2024), demolished it with public funds, and sold the cleared lot for $7,500 to a preferred developer under a blanket authorization, with no conflict-disclosure rule covering the transaction.
- **HOA self-management** — Land Bank staff (its attorney, executive director, and a real estate specialist) control the HOA boards for Genesee Pointe and Pointe West, while the Land Bank itself pays HOA dues on its own unsold units at $165/month per unit.
- **Structural conflict of interest** — the same County Treasurer who supplies the Land Bank's tax-foreclosed inventory chairs the board that decides its disposition.

- **Alan Fox** (Chair, ex officio) — Ingham County Treasurer; controls the tax-foreclosure pipeline supplying Land Bank inventory. Co-owned Practical Political Consulting with fellow board member Mark Grebner before becoming Treasurer.
- **Rawley Van Fossen** (Treasurer) — City of Lansing Director of Economic Development and Planning; issues demolition permits on properties the Land Bank sells; former CAHP executive director, and CAHP is a preferred Land Bank partner.
- **Emma Henry** (Member) — Executive Director, Capital Area Housing Partnership, which receives below-market Land Bank parcels; also chairs the Lansing Housing Commission, forming a three-way circular arrangement across Cases 7 and 8.
- **Mark Grebner** — board member; former business co-owner with Alan Fox prior to Fox's Treasurer role.

- Require competitive bidding for all property dispositions above a defined threshold, with minimum 30-day public notice.
- Prohibit board members from simultaneously holding roles that supply, receive, or permit Land Bank properties.
- Mandate income-restricted affordability covenants on all properties renovated using public grants.
- Commission a forensic audit of grant expenditures, modeled on the Detroit Land Bank audit (2020), which uncovered $50.8M in unapproved payments.
- Establish a public dashboard showing every parcel acquired, held, demolished, and sold, with dates, prices, and buyer identities.
- Set a moratorium on demolitions until a housing creation plan with measurable unit targets is adopted.

### Case 9: The Chamber/PAC Electoral Loop

*This case is different from the others: it is not a single development but the mechanism that has shaped the outcome of every case above.*

The Lansing Regional Chamber PAC (LRC-PAC) exists explicitly to elect officials to city council, township boards, and the state legislature who align with Chamber member priorities. **Six of eight sitting Lansing City Council members have accepted LRC-PAC money.** Only Ryan Kost and Adam Hussain have not. PAC donors are corporate lawyers, developers, lobbyists, and utility executives; independent neighborhood businesses and residents contribute essentially none of the funding.

**2025 election results:** six of seven LRC-PAC-endorsed candidates won — including Mayor Schor's re-election and four City Council seats. The Chamber also helped elect six of nine members of the Lansing City Charter Commission that rewrote the city's governing document.

**The manufactured consent case:** on a 2026 downtown data center rezoning, a PR firm submitted at least eleven near-identical "Vote YES" letters — nine in a single day, six within fourteen minutes of each other. The firm's partner sits on the Chamber's PAC endorsement committee while being paid to promote the project to the council that committee funds.

**The loop:** the same body that funds the council also endorses and campaigns for council members who vote on tax breaks, brownfield captures, lease approvals, and asset dispositions that Chamber donors seek. This is the Chamber's own stated mission — its FAQ describes LRC-PAC's purpose as "maintaining a pro-business outlook among elected officials."

#### The accounting gap:
This case has no benefit/cost columns in the usual sense, because its function is to determine the benefit/cost split in every other case. It is the reason the Shuffle sale passed 6-1, the reason the LHC board has the composition it has, and the reason a binding neighborhood council or independent reappraisal requirement has not yet been enacted despite being a simple council-majority action. **Until this loop is broken or counterbalanced, every other item in this accounting will keep recurring in new forms.**

### Case 10: Ingham Medical / Sparrow — From County Ward to Academic Health Conglomerate (1913–2026)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **113 years of continuous hospital care** on the Ingham Medical side, beginning as a public tuberculosis facility and evolving into general acute care.
- **129 years of continuous hospital care** on the Sparrow side, born from a private women's philanthropic initiative that filled a real gap after Lansing's only prior hospital (Sisters of Charity) closed in 1893.
- **A blocked predatory takeover.** In 1996, Michigan Attorney General Frank Kelley sued to stop Columbia/HCA — the for-profit chain that would later plead guilty to 14 federal felonies and pay $1.7 billion in the largest healthcare fraud settlement in U.S. history — from buying 50% of the merged Lansing hospital system. The suit succeeded; Lansing's hospital joined Michigan-based nonprofit McLaren instead of the company that turned out to be a criminal enterprise. This is a genuine case of state government protecting a charitable asset against extraction.
- **$800 million in pledged UM capital investment** in Sparrow over eight years, funding facility upgrades a stand-alone regional system might not have been able to afford.
- **New academic affiliations** (MSU medicine programs at McLaren; UM Health-Michigan Medicine's research and specialty depth at Sparrow) that arguably improved clinical capability beyond what either standalone Lansing system could offer.

#### What it actually cost:
- **Public ownership ended in 1992 with no public vote.** Ingham County sold Ingham Medical Center — a facility it had built and owned since 1913 with public money — to form Michigan Capital Healthcare. No ballot question, no documented public benefit requirement attached, as far as the record shows so far.
- **Governance moved out of the county, twice.** 1997: to McLaren, headquartered in Grand Blanc/Flint. 2023: Sparrow's ultimate corporate authority moved to the University of Michigan Board of Regents in Ann Arbor. Neither of Lansing's two hospital systems is now governed by anyone accountable to a Lansing or Ingham County ballot.
- **The physical evidence is being erased — twice, on both sides of this case.** The 1930 McLaren building — literally constructed with Ingham County public funds as the TB sanitorium's flagship structure — is currently being demolished (2024) to make way for "greenspace and future redevelopment." And on the Sparrow side: **Lansing Eastern High School** — Lansing School District's longest-continuously-operating school, open 91 years from 1928 to its final class in 2019 — was sold to Sparrow in a **unanimous January 2016 school board vote for $2.475 million** (18 acres), passed to UM Health via the 2023 merger, and demolished in early 2025 to clear the site for a new $83–97 million, 64-bed psychiatric hospital (sources differ on the exact figure between the project's initial announcement and its final approved budget). Jerry Norris is a Lansing Eastern alumnus; this is not an abstract case for him — it's the building where his own wrestling scholarship path was built, and it's now gone.

  **The precise finding here is sharper than "the public didn't want to sell it":** the 2016 sale itself drew little public opposition at the time, and there's a genuine documented public benefit to weigh in — voters approved a **$120 million bond that same year** which funded a modernized replacement Eastern inside the former Pattengill Middle School, and the old building reportedly needed $60–80 million in code repairs it was never going to get as a functioning school. The sharper problem is timing: in **2013**, three years before any sale vote occurred, then-Mayor Virg Bernero floated a performing-arts-center concept for Eastern's historic auditorium at a public preservation event — then, when asked afterward, admitted the Sparrow sale was already a **"done deal."** The public conversation about saving the building predates the actual decision being real by three years; the decision was privately settled before the public discussion of alternatives ever happened. Real opposition only organized around the **2024–2025 demolition**, not the original sale — by which point City Council member Peter Spadafore, who had been school board president at the time of the 2016 sale vote, explicitly declined to support a historic-district study, saying "we had 12 years to do that" and nothing happened until UM-Sparrow's demolition plan forced the issue. Sparrow's own original 2015 purchase bid stated it would "maintain aspects of the historic value" of the facility while separately acknowledging the building would be "razed for all practical purposes" — both claims made in the same document.

  A community preservation coalition (the Coalition to Preserve Eastern High School and Promote Mental Health, led in part by local historian Linda Peckham) fought specifically to save the building's west wing and auditorium on Pennsylvania Avenue, not to block the mental health facility itself. City Council rejected the historic-designation effort that would have preserved at least the façade — City Pulse reported this happened "under pressure from both UM-Sparrow and trade unions," a detail worth naming alongside Case 12's Jeremy Garza/Local 333 conflict as a second possible instance of union interest in construction work aligning against a preservation effort, though the specific union(s) involved here haven't been confirmed. Mayor Schor stated the city was legally obligated to issue the demolition permit once requirements were met, and warned that blocking it would likely trigger a lawsuit at taxpayer expense — demolition proceeded without requiring either a Council or mayoral vote. UM-Sparrow's stated remediation: a "remembrance garden" near the site, still pending its own City Council approval as of early 2026. **Rawley Van Fossen — already named in Cases 7 and 8 — appears here too**, as the City of Lansing's Director of Economic Development and Planning, confirming him as the individual with the broadest cross-case presence documented anywhere in this project. **Ryan Kost** chaired a council effort to preserve the building, his fourth appearance in this document (Cases 6, 9, 10, 12). The public origin of both institutions — a county TB ward, a 91-year-old public school — is disappearing from the built landscape on both sides of this case at the same time it's disappearing from institutional memory.
- **A documented post-merger casualty at Sparrow.** UM Health Plan (formerly Sparrow's Physicians Health Plan), covering 64,000 Lansing-area members, was wound down in 2025 after UM failed to find a buyer — a direct, quantifiable harm to local coverage access following the merger, distinct from the "no purchase price disclosed" framing UM and Sparrow leadership used publicly.
- **The "member substitution agreement" structure.** Sparrow's 2023 deal with UM was explicitly structured with no purchase price — a known technique nonprofit-to-nonprofit mergers use partly to avoid the closer regulatory and public-disclosure scrutiny an actual sale would trigger. Worth checking whether Michigan's AG conducted the kind of charitable-conversion review it applied in 1996 against Columbia/HCA, or whether the "no money changes hands" structure sidestepped that entirely.

#### The accounting gap:
Unlike most cases in this document, this one doesn't have a clean villain — the 1996 Columbia/HCA block was a genuine public win, and $800M in committed capital is a real number, not a PR gesture. But the throughline still holds: an asset built and owned by county government in 1913, funded by public dollars for half a century, has ended up — through two separate, decades-apart transactions — controlled by out-of-region institutions (Grand Blanc corporate headquarters; Ann Arbor university regents) with no local electorate able to hold either accountable the way voters could once hold county commissioners accountable for hospital decisions. The near-miss with Columbia/HCA proves the AG's office *can* intervene to protect a charitable asset when it wants to — which raises the question of why no comparable public review appears to have accompanied either the quieter 1992 county sale or the 2023 UM merger's zero-dollar structure.

**Governance note:** This case is the clearest example in the Full Accounting of the "starve the failing systems" pattern working in reverse — public capacity was not starved by neglect, but affirmatively transferred, sale by sale, merger by merger, into private and now non-local hands, each time framed publicly as growth and improved care rather than loss of public control.

### Case 11: NOVA Lansing Housing Initiative / ModPods (2025–2026, ongoing)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **A stated design intent that goes beyond shelter.** The city's own framing describes the program as "a bridge from homelessness to permanent housing" — 50 modular units housing approximately 66 people, with heating/cooling, secure entry, ADA-compliant options, a central bathroom/shower facility, a computer lab, and case management through Detroit Rescue Mission Ministries (DRMM), a Detroit-based nonprofit operating since 1909 with an existing track record running Lansing's emergency winter warming center.
- **A genuine funding partnership.** Ingham County contributed $600,000 through its Housing Trust toward upfront costs — a real cross-jurisdictional commitment, not just a city-only initiative.
- **A public, iterative site-selection process.** 48 properties were evaluated, narrowed to 5, presented at multiple public meetings (Letts Community Center, Foster Community Center) across Nov.–Dec. 2025, with public comment genuinely shaping the outcome — parks were removed from consideration after neighborhood opposition, landing on the Ingham County Human Services building parking lot at 5303 S. Cedar St.

#### What it actually cost:
- **Cost escalation with no fixed ceiling in sight.** Initial estimate: $750,000. By July 2026: City Council approved $1,925,900 for site prep and construction, plus $952,335/year projected operating costs. By April 2026, a separate budget discussion put the first-year all-in total closer to **$2.9 million** — city officials said the two figures "are not directly comparable" because the earlier number only reflected ongoing operations, not one-time startup costs. That explanation may be accurate, but it also means no single, stable public number has existed for this project at any point in its life — every update has been a new, larger figure.
- **No fixed timeline, over a year in.** The city's own department (Human Relations and Community Services, under Director Kimberly Coleman) has been working on this "since August 2025." As of the August 2026 update, WLNS reported "no exact timeline for installation" — over a year of planning with construction still not begun.
- **Leadership discontinuity mid-project.** Kim Coleman, the HRCS Director who had led NOVA planning since August 2025, announced her resignation Feb. 2, 2026 — effective Feb. 13 — roughly six months into the project's most consequential planning year and well before construction began. Mayor Schor's office cited personal reasons ("spend more time with family, pursue other interests"); no wrongdoing has been reported in connection with her departure. Notably, the city did not fully hand the project to new leadership: Coleman was retained specifically to continue coordinating the NOVA initiative in a non-director capacity, while Deputy Director Delvata Moses became interim HRCS director for everything else. That is an unusual structure — a flagship program continuing to run through a departed department head's personal involvement rather than through the department's institutional continuity. Worth noting for context: Coleman herself had been hired in Feb. 2020 to replace a prior HRCS director, Joan Jackson Johnson, who was placed on leave and retired amid a HUD audit that found the department had awarded funds to charities connected to her without proper disclosure. That is a different and more serious situation than Coleman's departure — no comparable finding has been reported against Coleman — but it means the department's last two full-time directors have both left mid-tenure under some form of unplanned circumstance, one under audit findings and one during her signature project's build-out.
- **Coleman's defense of the sole-bidder outcome, as recounted by Jerry Norris (paraphrased, not a direct quote):** when the sole-bidder question was raised, Coleman — by this point acting in her post-resignation NOVA-coordinator capacity — defended Detroit Rescue Mission Ministries' qualification by pointing to the organization's long institutional history managing unhoused populations, cited as roughly 115 years. DRMM's own organizational history dates its founding to 1909, which is approximately 117 years before 2026 — close enough to the figure given that this reads as a rounded, in-the-moment reference rather than a precise claim, but it should be treated as Coleman's paraphrased framing, not verified language from her, until an exact quote is available.
- **The transition-out plan, as documented directly by Jerry Norris at the Aug. 18, 2026 community update meeting, Foster Community Center:** when asked what happens to residents after their 6-month-to-2-year stay, the answer given was a website landlords can register properties on. One HRCS Advisory Board member compared the transition to "going off to college" — figuring out laundry, figuring out transportation — an analogy that assumes a financial cushion and a family safety net that, by definition, most people exiting homelessness do not have. When asked directly, the full panel affirmed that housing is a human right. The stated value and the operational mechanism do not match.
- **The pods themselves are secondhand.** They were purchased from a failed Kalamazoo nonprofit's own attempt at this model, at auction — not designed and built for Lansing's program from the outset.

#### The accounting gap — and the direct link to Case 10:
During the Dec. 2025 public comment period, at least one resident raised a fact that connects this case directly to Case 10: **McLaren offered its vacant Greenlawn hospital building to the city for free, approximately five years ago.** That building — the same 1930 structure built with Ingham County public money as the original tuberculosis sanitorium, now sitting vacant and, per Case 10, being demolished — was reportedly available to the city at no cost around 2021, while the city has since spent an escalating multi-million-dollar sum standing up a temporary outdoor pod community from scratch. If that offer was real and lapsed, this is not a hypothetical missed opportunity — it is a documented instance of the exact drift pattern this entire accounting is built to track: a public asset with obvious reuse potential was allowed to sit idle and is now being erased, while new public money is spent solving an overlapping problem somewhere else. **This needs verification before it goes any further** — confirm the offer's terms, timing, and why it wasn't pursued, directly with the city or with McLaren.

Separately: the program was sold to the public and to Council on the premise of case management and a genuine path to permanent housing. A year into planning, the only documented answer to "what happens next" for residents is a passive landlord-registry website. Process has occurred — public meetings, board votes, a funding partnership, a procurement process — but as with NOVA's own predecessor pattern already noted elsewhere in this project, **process without a binding delivery mechanism still produces drift.** Nothing here suggests bad faith. It suggests an institution that is good at running meetings and still has not answered its own central design question.

**Governance note:** This is the clearest live instance in the entire Full Accounting of "committed, not yet spent" — public money has been approved and partially disbursed, a public-private partnership structure is in place, and yet the single most important question for anyone who will actually live in this program — what happens when your time here ends — does not yet have a real answer. Advisory board participation, extensive public comment, and a competitive-in-name procurement process do not, by themselves, guarantee that the resulting program serves the population it was built for.

### Case 12: Deep Green / The Data Center Moratorium and the I-HVY Loophole (2025–2026, ongoing)

#### What the community genuinely received:
- **A real, hard-won procedural win.** After UK-based Deep Green withdrew its downtown data center proposal in April 2026, City Council — led by Ward 1's Ryan Kost working with Ward 2's Dr. Deyanira Nevarez Martinez — passed a 182-day moratorium on new data center permits and zoning amendments, 7–1, on July 13, 2026. This is a genuine, documented instance of the council acting on public pressure rather than deferring to the Chamber's "closed sign" framing — worth stating plainly as a win, the same way Case 6's ballot rejection was.
- **Real commitments extracted from Deep Green during negotiation**, even though the project ultimately died: closed-loop cooling limiting water use to roughly 15 households, upfront payment of all energy infrastructure costs, standard published utility rates rather than a discounted special contract, and compliance with downtown noise and aesthetic standards. Whatever the project's ultimate fate, the negotiation process did extract real, specific, binding-sounding concessions — a template worth studying for what community leverage during the application phase can produce, even on a project that ultimately fails.

#### What it actually cost — and the loophole that survived:
- **The moratorium does not close the door it was built to close.** Under Lansing's existing zoning code, a data center is a **by-right use in I-HVY (Heavy Industrial) districts** — meaning a developer who buys a privately owned I-HVY parcel can pull a building permit directly from the Building Department with **no Council vote, no Planning Commission vote, and no public hearing**. The moratorium pauses new permits and rezonings for 182 days (effective July 27, 2026, expiring **January 25, 2027**) but does not touch this underlying by-right pathway once the moratorium lapses.
- **The ordinance that would actually close the loophole has been stuck in committee with no hearing scheduled**, as of the most recent reporting (Rhinoceros Media, ~30 days after referral). One committee meeting that could have taken it up was removed from the public calendar with no cancellation notice; a second meeting in the same window handled an unrelated tax-break application instead.
- **A direct, documented conflict of interest sits at the chokepoint.** The chair of the Committee on Development and Planning — the committee where the closing ordinance is stalled — is **Jeremy Garza**, at-large councilmember and Vice President of UA Plumbers and Pipefitters Local 333. Local 333 **publicly backed the original Deep Green project**. Garza was also the lone "no" vote against the moratorium itself. The same person sits at the exact procedural pinch point where the permanent fix is currently stalled, and has both a labor-affiliation stake in future data center construction work and a voting record opposing the pause itself.
- **Nineteen other Michigan municipalities have already closed this exact loophole**, including Mason, directly south of Lansing. Lansing has not, meaning that for every week the closing ordinance sits without a hearing, any privately owned I-HVY parcel in the city remains open to exactly the kind of no-vote, no-hearing data center siting the moratorium was publicly sold as preventing.

#### The energy question underneath it — and where Bloom Energy fits:
This case sits inside a much larger, fast-moving statewide pattern that any Lansing-specific account has to be read against. Michigan's biggest current data center commitment — the $7 billion, 1.4-gigawatt Oracle/OpenAI/Related Digital "Stargate" campus in Saline Township, approved by the MPSC in a contested 3–0 vote on December 18, 2025 — is, as currently structured, **grid-supplied by DTE using existing resources plus data-center-funded battery storage**, not on-site fuel cells. DTE's own CEO has stated that *additional* capacity beyond that 1.4 GW would likely require new generation, up to and including a new combined-cycle gas plant replacing the Monroe coal plant.

Separately — and this is the connection worth tracking closely rather than asserting as settled fact — **Oracle**, the same company anchoring the Saline Township project, signed a **master agreement with Bloom Energy in April 2026 for up to 2.8 gigawatts of solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) capacity** to power its AI infrastructure globally, part of a broader $7.65 billion, 90-day surge in Bloom's data-center contracts nationally (Bloom/Brookfield's financing partnership alone expanded from $5B to $25B in mid-2026). The entire commercial logic of this fuel-cell wave, industry-wide, is that **on-site fuel cells let a data center bypass the multi-year grid interconnection queue entirely** — exactly the kind of speed advantage that would make an I-HVY by-right parcel in Lansing, with no permitting delay and no on-site generation review requirement, an unusually attractive site for precisely this technology.

**No Michigan-specific Bloom Energy deployment has been confirmed in any source found for this case as of this writing.** This is flagged as the single most important thing to monitor, not as an established fact: if Oracle or any Bloom-partnered developer pursues a Lansing I-HVY parcel before the closing ordinance passes, the by-right pathway plus fuel-cell speed-to-power would combine to produce a facility with essentially no local review at any stage — zoning, energy sourcing, or water use. That is the specific, concrete risk this case exists to track.

#### The accounting gap:
The moratorium is real progress and deserves to be credited as such. But a temporary pause that expires on a fixed date (Jan. 25, 2027), sitting on top of a permanent loophole whose closing ordinance is stalled in a committee chaired by someone with a direct interest in the outcome, is close to a textbook instance of this document's recurring finding: **process without a binding consequence still produces drift.** The moratorium buys time. It does not yet buy a structural fix, and the clock on it is running.

**Governance note:** This is also a clean, small-scale instance of the Chamber/PAC electoral loop's broader logic (Case 9) operating through a different channel — not campaign contributions this time, but a union's institutional interest in construction work sitting directly on the committee that controls the relevant ordinance's fate.

### Case 13: The Assessor and Board of Review — A Self-Review Loop (ongoing)

**Header stats:** 1 appointer for both the valuer and the appeals body · ~200+ mayoral appointments across all Lansing boards and commissions, per a 2024 charter commission candidate's own public statement · 0 documented independent path for a homeowner to appeal to a body free of the appointing executive's influence, short of the state-level Michigan Tax Tribunal · 2025 charter revision passed with no change to this specific structure

**Summary:** Every property owner in Lansing who believes their tax assessment is wrong has exactly one local body to appeal to before the state: the Board of Review. Both the person who set the number being disputed (the Assessor) and the body deciding whether that number was fair (the Board of Review) are appointed by the same person — the Mayor. This is not a hidden or informal arrangement; it is written directly into the City Charter. It has not been changed by the 2025 charter revision, which added real accountability infrastructure elsewhere (an internal auditor, a mandatory strategic plan) but left this specific structure untouched.

#### The Seven ICA Cooperative Principles — applied

**1. Voluntary and Open Membership.** Every property owner is a mandatory participant in this system with no opt-out — appealing an assessment is the only lawful way to contest a tax bill, and the appeal body is not independent of the office that set the number.

**2. Democratic Member Control.** Property owners have no vote in who assesses their property or who hears their appeal. Both roles are filled by mayoral appointment. *Evidence: City Charter — "the Mayor shall, after consultation with the Council, appoint... an Assessor."*

**3. Member Economic Participation.** The financial stakes are entirely one-directional: a higher assessment means a higher tax bill for the owner and more revenue for the city whose chief executive appointed the assessor. The owner has no comparable leverage in the relationship.

**4. Autonomy and Independence.** The Board of Review is not autonomous from the executive branch it is meant to check. *Evidence: a 2024 candidate for Lansing's Charter Commission stated publicly, on the record with WKAR, that the Mayor appoints "all 200+ individuals" across the city's boards and commissions — a scale of concentrated appointment power a candidate for the very body reviewing the charter considered serious enough to campaign on changing.*

**5. Education, Training, and Information.** Property owners are not given comparative data on how their appointed Board of Review's reversal rate compares to bodies with independent appointment structures elsewhere in Michigan — this project has not yet located such a comparison, and it's flagged as an open research thread below rather than assumed.

**6. Cooperation Among Cooperatives.** No alternative dispute-resolution structure — a resident-elected review board, an independently appointed panel — appears to have been proposed or considered during the 2025 charter revision process, based on the record reviewed for this case so far.

**7. Concern for Community.** The property tax uncapping mechanism already documented elsewhere in this project (Proposal A, 1994) means assessment disputes disproportionately affect properties that have recently changed hands — often lower-income buyers and renters whose landlords pass through the cost — making an independent appeals structure more consequential for exactly the population this project has documented as least protected elsewhere (see the property tax structural analysis in the Timeline's 2026 research additions).

#### The Five Ownership Questions

| Question | Structure | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns it? | The Assessor's office and the Board of Review are both City of Lansing offices | MIXED — public in form |
| Who has power? | The Mayor, who appoints both the person setting the valuation and the body reviewing it | EXTRACTIVE |
| Who benefits? | The city's revenue base benefits from higher assessments; the appointing executive controls both ends of the process that produces that revenue | EXTRACTIVE |
| Who does the work? | City assessing staff; Board of Review members serving in what is functionally a part-time, low-visibility role | MIXED |
| Who makes the rules? | Michigan's General Property Tax Act (1893, as amended) sets the statewide framework; the City Charter sets who fills the local roles inside it | MIXED — state law provides some outside constraint, but the local appointment structure is entirely within the Mayor's control |

#### The Four Bottom Lines

- **People — negative.** A homeowner disputing an assessment has no local avenue free of the appointing executive's influence; the only fully independent recourse is the Michigan Tax Tribunal, a state body requiring a separate filing, deadline, and often legal or appraisal expertise a typical homeowner doesn't have on hand.
- **Planet — not applicable.** This case does not have a clear environmental dimension.
- **Prosperity — negative, concentrated on recent buyers.** Combined with the Proposal A uncapping mechanism already documented in this project, an assessment dispute lands hardest on households who've most recently purchased or whose landlord has most recently purchased — exactly the population with the least standing capital to absorb an incorrect valuation while it's being appealed.
- **Purpose — insufficient.** The Board of Review's statutory purpose is independent review. A structure where the reviewing body is appointed by the same executive whose office benefits from higher assessments does not, on its face, deliver on that purpose, regardless of whether any individual Board member has acted in bad faith — this is a structural finding, not an accusation against any named person.

#### What the community genuinely received:
- A functioning, statutorily compliant assessment and appeals process that meets Michigan's General Property Tax Act requirements — this is not an illegal or rogue structure, it is the standard mayoral-appointment model used in many Michigan municipalities.
- The 2025 charter revision's new accountability infrastructure (the independent internal auditor, the mandatory strategic plan) does apply generally across city government and could, in principle, eventually surface findings relevant to assessment practices, even though it didn't restructure this specific appointment mechanism directly.

#### The pattern — what it actually costs:
- **The appointer-appointee loop.** The Mayor appoints the Assessor, whose valuations determine tax revenue. The Mayor also appoints the Board of Review, the first and only local body a property owner can appeal to. The same executive sits, in effect, on both sides of every dispute a resident might bring.
- **No independent local check.** Unlike Michigan municipalities that use council appointment or resident election for review boards (a structure other Michigan cities are documented as using), Lansing's model concentrates both appointments in the same office.
- **Scale.** This is not an isolated instance — it's one case inside a much larger pattern: a 2024 charter commission candidate specifically flagged that the Mayor appoints roughly 200+ individuals across all of Lansing's boards and commissions combined, campaigning explicitly on splitting that appointment power with the City Council. The Assessor/Board of Review relationship is a specific, high-stakes instance of a much broader structural concentration.
- **Untouched by the 2025 reform.** The charter revision that added the internal auditor and strategic-plan requirements — both documented elsewhere in this project as still not fully implemented as of mid-2026 — did not restructure board and commission appointment authority. This specific capture point was not part of what got fixed.

#### Named conflicts of interest:
No individual is named in this case as having acted improperly — this is a structural finding about the appointment mechanism itself, not an allegation against the current Assessor, the current Mayor, or any sitting Board of Review member. That distinction is deliberate: the case for reform here doesn't depend on anyone having done anything wrong, which is arguably what makes it a cleaner example of "process without binding independence" than cases involving specific bad-faith actors.

#### Recommendations:
- Restructure Board of Review appointments to require City Council confirmation independent of the Mayor's nomination, or move to a City Council appointment model outright, following the pattern already used for some other Michigan municipalities' review boards.
- Publish the Board of Review's annual reversal rate (how often appealed assessments are adjusted) as a standing public metric, the same way the charter's new dashboard requirements apply to other city financial data.
- Include board-and-commission appointment authority explicitly in the next charter revision cycle (2041, per the current charter's amendment schedule) as a standing item, given a sitting charter candidate already flagged it as a priority in 2024 without it being resolved in the 2025 revision.

## The Throughline

Every phase moves public assets and public risk in the same direction.

Industry extracts value and externalizes pollution onto a neighborhood. The highway erases that neighborhood instead of addressing the harm. The company leaves through bankruptcy and the cleanup bill lands on the public. The cleaned-up riverfront gets repackaged as private amenity, financed by corporate donors and sold off in pieces through leases with purchase options. Public housing built with federal money over 80 years gets converted into private limited partnerships at $72K per unit while the families who lived there face eviction. A Chamber-funded council approves each step.

The mechanism is not corruption in the cinematic sense. It is a small, identifiable network of people sitting on both sides of every transaction simultaneously — and an electoral pipeline that keeps them there. Doug Fleming signs both sides of a deal. Ashlee Barker votes on her employer's profits. Emma Henry chairs the board that sends contracts to the nonprofit she runs. The Chamber funds the council that approves what its donors want.

**The people who absorbed the original cost — the displaced families of the I-496 corridor, the public housing tenants, the workers breathing coal plant emissions — are not the people capturing the value created by the riverfront's revitalization.** That is the pattern, across 125 years.

## The Plan Forward

The accounting above makes the case. The plan below is the response.

It is organized in three time horizons: **immediate** (actions available now, within existing authority), **near-term** (2–5 years, requiring policy change or cooperative capital), and **structural** (5–15 years, requiring governance change or legal framework shifts).

---

### Immediate Actions (now, within existing authority)

**1. Public Asset Disposition Watch**
Establish a standing tracker on lansing.love that flags — at the moment of proposal, not the moment of vote — any lease with a purchase option, any RAD or Section 18 conversion, any brownfield tax-abatement arrangement, any public land sale, and any inter-agency contract involving board members with overlapping roles. Source: public meeting agendas, FOIA requests, planning commission filings. Update weekly. The goal is lead time: the Shuffle's purchase option was locked in 2021. The community needs to see it in 2021, not 2026.

**2. Independent Reappraisal Requirement Campaign**
Advocate for a city council policy — not a charter amendment, just a council rule — requiring independent reappraisal and a mandatory 90-day public comment period before any publicly owned land sale can go to a ballot question. This is a procedural change that requires only a council majority. It would have changed the Shuffle timeline materially. Ryan Kost is the existing council ally; build the count from there.

**3. LHC Records Campaign**
Continue and escalate the FOIA fight with documented public support. The five requests and $4,430 in fees with zero records produced is a documented accountability failure. Make it a public campaign. Every dollar of fees and every day of delay is a data point for the argument that the governance structure is actively hiding what happened to public housing stock.

**4. Rhinoceros Media sourcing integration**
Every claim on lansing.love links directly to Rhinoceros's sourced investigative work. The site is the framework; Rhinoceros is the evidentiary base. Make the linkage explicit and keep it current as new investigations publish.

### Near-Term Actions (2–5 years, requiring policy change or cooperative capital)

**5. Cooperative Development Fund — seed capital**
The single most important structural gap is the absence of patient capital. Detroit Rising had $4.2M and five years. The Parks Board had a recommendation. A cooperative or community land trust development fund — even at $500K to start — changes that dynamic. The Fledge Foundation is the existing vehicle. Sunshine House is the existing proof of concept. The near-term goal: capitalize a fund sufficient to make a credible alternative bid the next time a public asset comes up for disposition.

Sources to explore: CDFI financing, Michigan Economic Development Corporation cooperative programs, community investment notes sold to Lansing residents, philanthropic alignment with foundations already funding Rotary Park-type projects (reframe from talent-retention to community-ownership).

**6. Community Land Trust for RACER sites**
The three former GM sites (Plants 2, 3, and 6 — 234 acres, heavy industrial, partially remediated) represent the largest undeveloped land bank in central Lansing. A developer has been waiting since 2016. The window to attach community-benefit or cooperative-ownership requirements is now, before that deal finalizes.

Concrete ask: require any RACER Trust land sale in Lansing to include a Community Benefit Agreement with binding provisions for: affordable housing percentage, local hiring preference, cooperative business space, environmental monitoring rights, and community representation on any development governance body.

**7. LHC Cooperative Housing Conversion**
As the remaining 66 LHC-owned units come up for capital needs or conversion decisions, establish a community land trust or limited-equity cooperative as the preferred alternative to RAD/Section 18 private partnership conversion. The Sunshine House rent-to-own model is the template. A limited-equity cooperative gives residents equity, governance rights, and protection from eviction in ways that neither public housing tenancy nor private rental provides.

Immediate version: advocate for LHC's next disposition decision to include a community land trust bid. If LHC won't consider it, document that refusal as an accountability finding.

The Shuffle sale passed 6-1. Kost and Hussain are the current non-PAC-funded members. The path to a different outcome on the next equivalent decision requires three more aligned votes. The realistic path:

**9. Sparrow / UMH Community Benefit Agreement**
UMH-Sparrow is building a $97M psychiatric hospital and a $32M health center in Lansing. Both are receiving some form of public support (tax abatement, zoning approval, public infrastructure). A community benefit agreement is a standard tool — typically covering local hiring, charity care commitments, community health program funding, and wage floors. File a formal request to City Council to require a CBA before any additional public benefit is extended to UMH-Sparrow expansion. Use the documented ratio of capital-for-buildings vs. contract-bad-faith as the evidentiary basis.

### Structural Actions (5–15 years, requiring governance framework changes)

**10. Binding Neighborhood Councils**
The new city charter (effective January 1, 2026) created accountability infrastructure but no binding neighborhood authority. The next charter revision opportunity is 2041. That's 15 years. The interim strategy:
- Advocate for council-passed neighborhood council ordinances with specific binding authorities: advisory review rights on lease terms above a threshold dollar value, required response periods before public asset dispositions, and formal standing in planning commission proceedings.
- These don't require a charter amendment. They require a council majority willing to create them. That council majority requires the electoral work in item 8.

**11. Public Asset Disposition Policy — codified**
Turn the independent reappraisal campaign (item 2) into a codified city ordinance: any lease of city-owned property with a term exceeding 10 years must include City Council approval of any purchase option language before signing; any sale of city-owned land requires independent appraisal within 12 months of the vote date; any public land sale above $500K requires a 90-day public comment period with a formal community benefit review. Write the ordinance. Find a council sponsor. Build the public record.

**12. Cooperative Business Infrastructure**
The Fledge's ten-domain framework maps directly to the domains where monocentric governance has failed: housing, food, healthcare, energy, transportation. Build cooperative infrastructure in each domain — not all at once, but sequentially, using each proof of concept to fund the next. Priority order based on where the most immediate extraction is occurring:
- Housing: Sunshine House → CLT → cooperative conversion of next LHC assets
- Food: Urbandale Farm → food cooperative retail → land stewardship
- Energy: BWL accountability campaign → community solar → cooperative energy purchasing
- Technology: lansing.love infrastructure → community-owned broadband advocacy

**13. Environmental Justice Accounting — make it official**
Commission or co-produce with MSU or Rhinoceros an environmental justice audit of the I-496 corridor, the former GM plant sites, and the Eckert/Erickson service area: who lived there, what they were exposed to, what it cost them in health outcomes and property values, what remediation has been done and at whose expense. Make this the evidentiary basis for a formal reparative investment demand — not a lawsuit, but a documented public claim that the cost accounting is incomplete and specific remediation investments are owed to specific communities. The I-496 cap study grant ($1M, January 2025) is a precedent. Leverage it.

## The Core Argument

*Editorial note, not part of the published text below: this section is preserved exactly as written for verbatim publication to lansing.love. Two factual claims inside it are now out of date given this session's additions and need your decision, not a silent edit: (1) "$363M" — the current documented total is ~$1.36B nominal / ~$1.47B 2026-adjusted, once Case 5's $291M and Case 10's $800M pledge are included in the ledger as they should be; (2) "across 125 years" — with Case 0 now covering 1819–1862, the actual span this document covers is closer to 165–207 years depending on which starting point you count from. The question is not whether Lansing should have had an auto industry, a power utility, a housing commission, or a city market. The question is whether those institutions should have been governed in a way that gave affected communities a binding seat at the table before the decisions that harmed them were made.

The accounting above shows that across 125 years, the answer was consistently no — and the documented cost of that no is at minimum $363M in public cleanup and infrastructure remediation, plus unquantified generational wealth destruction, health burdens, and displacement harm concentrated on the communities with the least power to prevent it.

Polycentric governance doesn't claim that all of those harms were avoidable. It claims that a governance structure with binding neighborhood councils, community land trusts, cooperative ownership mechanisms, and participatory budgeting would have distributed both the benefits and the costs more equitably — and would have created institutional checks that made the most egregious decisions (the I-496 routing, the LHC dual-signature deals, the Shuffle purchase option) harder to execute without community knowledge and consent.

The plan forward is not a revolution. It is a patient, sequential build: one cooperative, one council vote, one policy change, one community land trust at a time — using each proof of concept to fund and justify the next, until the governance structure of this city reflects the people who actually live here rather than the network of boards and donors who currently decide for them.

That is what The Fledge is building. That is what lansing.love is documenting. That is what Rhinoceros Media is making impossible to ignore.

The loop only breaks when the community has a binding seat at the table before the deal is signed.
