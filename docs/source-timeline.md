# Lansing Raised Me — The Merged Timeline
## A City and a Family, Together from the Beginning
### Working document — July 2026

*This timeline weaves the documented history of Lansing with the documented and remembered history of the Norris, Arnold, and DeWeese families across six generations. Documented facts are stated as facts. Family memory and oral history are noted as such. Both belong in the book.*

## DEEP ROOTS: Before Lansing Was Lansing

### ~1690 — The DeWeese family arrives in Pennsylvania
Old Dutch settlers, Quakers, land in Germantown, Pennsylvania — among the earliest European arrivals in the Delaware Valley. The family is part of a tight-knit Dutch Quaker community in the same region as the Lenape people, who have been on that land for thousands of years. *Family rumor, passed down: somewhere in this era, a Dutch DeWeese family who could not have children takes in a Native indigenous child and raises the child as their own. The rumor is on Margot's side of the family — Jerry's mother. Whether true or not, it is the story that was told. It lives in the book as family memory.*

### ~1739 — William Dewees Jr. born, Pennsylvania
Son of the Philadelphia County sheriff. He will become co-owner of a forge on a creek called Valley Forge, and one of the most important figures in the founding of the republic.

### 1777 — Valley Forge Winter
William Dewees Jr. and his cousin David Potts co-own and operate the iron forge on Valley Creek, Pennsylvania. General George Washington's Continental Army encamps at Valley Forge in the brutal winter of 1777–1778. William Dewees Jr. — a Quaker who becomes a colonel in the Pennsylvania militia — and Isaac Potts devote a large part of the forge's production to the war effort, supplying munitions to Washington's army. Dewees corresponds directly with Washington. The old stone Dewees house still stands in Valley Forge National Historical Park.

*Margot (DeWeese) Norris — Jerry's biological mother — is a Daughter of the American Revolution. Her lineage traces through the DeWeese family to a documented Revolutionary War ancestor. The Norrises of Lansing carry the blood of the family that helped forge the nation, alongside the probable blood of the Native people that nation displaced.*

### 1819 — The Treaty of Saginaw
The land that will become Lansing, East Lansing, and the surrounding region enters U.S. federal control through the Treaty of Saginaw — negotiated with the Saginaw Band of Chippewa and the broader Anishinaabeg, the Three Fires Confederacy of Ojibwe, Odawa, and Potawatomi peoples who have lived on this land for generations before any settler arrives. Michigan State University's own current land acknowledgment materials — the institution that will eventually rise on this same ground — state plainly that the treaty was negotiated "under coercive or violent circumstances," and that "settler and Indigenous signatories understood the terms of the treaties in starkly different terms." Two more treaties in 1836, including the Treaty of Washington, complete the cession of the surrounding region.

This is the actual beginning of Lansing's own story — 16 years before Biddle City, 28 years before Lansing becomes state capital, 38 years before the college that becomes MSU opens its doors. Everything that follows in this timeline happens on this ground, transferred under terms one side did not freely choose.

## PART ONE: The City Takes Shape (1835–1900)

### 1835 — The Biddle City Land Scam
New York land speculators sell lots in "Biddle City" — a settlement they describe as a promising town in Michigan Territory — to buyers who have never seen it. When buyers arrive, they find a frozen wilderness. Nothing but trees. The original extraction: land sold to people who don't know what they're buying, by people who aren't there and won't bear the consequences. Lansing begins as a scam.

### 1847 — Lansing becomes state capital
The Michigan legislature, concerned that Detroit is too vulnerable to British attack near the Canadian border, votes to move the state capital. They choose Lansing — a tiny settlement in the wilderness that most legislators have never visited. The decision is made entirely at the center of power with no meaningful input from the people of Lansing. The pattern of governance that will define the city for the next 180 years is established in its first major political act.

### 1855–1862 — Michigan Agricultural College and the Morrill Act
In 1855, Governor Kinsley Bingham signs the bill creating the Agricultural College of the State of Michigan — the first institution in the United States to teach scientific agriculture, built explicitly to extend higher education "regardless of social class" at a time when college is almost exclusively a privilege of the wealthy. It becomes the direct model for the 1862 Morrill Act, the federal law that creates the entire American land-grant university system — more than 100 institutions nationally will eventually trace their funding to this model.

The Morrill Act funds these colleges by granting states federal public land, or land scrip where a state lacks sufficient public land within its own borders, to be sold with proceeds funding the colleges. Nationally, this system distributes land scrip drawn from roughly 10.7 million acres taken from 245 tribal nations, per Michigan State University's own American Indian and Indigenous Studies program. Michigan's specific land grant draws from parcels ceded in the 1819 Treaty of Saginaw and the 1836 treaties described above — the same land, transferred under the same coercive terms, now converted into the founding capital for what becomes a flagship public university two miles from where the state capitol will eventually stand.

An Anishinaabe "Indian Encampment" is documented on university archive maps as still present just south of the Red Cedar River when the first classes are held in 1857. Displacement is not a distant historical abstraction to the institution's founders — it is visibly ongoing on the same ground where students are beginning their first semester.

### ~1856–1860s — The Arnold family, Beaver Island, Lake Michigan
*Corrected dating: this entry was previously headed "~1800s" and placed in the Deep Roots section ahead of Biddle City (1835) — its own content describes events after 1856, roughly two decades later than that placement. Moved here to reflect that.*

The Arnold family — Thelma's people — are part of the Irish Catholic fishing community that settles Beaver Island after the fall of the Mormon kingdom of James Jesse Strang in 1856. They come primarily from Arranmore island in County Donegal, Ireland — an Irish-speaking island. They bring the Gaelic language to a Lake Michigan island 32 miles from the mainland. Irish is the dominant spoken language on Beaver Island until 1903.

**George T. Arnold** — documented in the Clarke Historical Library's biographical papers on Beaver Island as a significant figure in building the island community. Likely Thelma's grandfather or great-grandfather. Jerry remembers the family saying the name sounded like "teith or taft" — almost certainly George T. Arnold, George T.

*Family memory on Thelma's side: at some point, a grandmother whose husband was working on the other side of the island had a relationship with an Odawa man from Garden Island — directly across the water from Beaver Island — who helped her family. The Odawa had been on these islands for 300 years before the Irish arrived. What came of that relationship was carried forward quietly, the way these things were in small island communities. The indigenous ancestry rumor exists on both sides of Jerry's family — the DeWeese side and the Arnold side. Both are family memory, not documented fact. Both are true to the book.*

### 1859 — Lansing incorporated as a city
Seven square miles carved from Lansing Township. Boundaries: Douglas Avenue to the north, Wood and Regent Streets to the east, Mount Hope Avenue to the south, Jenison Avenue to the west. One city government for seven square miles. No neighborhood councils. No participatory mechanism. The template is set.

### 1885 — BWL founded by citizen bond vote
Lansing citizens approve a $100,000 bond to build a municipal water system. In 1892, they vote to municipalize electricity rather than pay private company rates. One of the few moments in Lansing's history when residents voted directly on infrastructure governance. It will be one of the last.

### 1897 — Ransom Olds founds the Olds Motor Vehicle Company
Ransom Eli Olds establishes his company in Lansing. The city's identity — its economy, its neighborhoods, its labor culture, its racial geography — will be shaped by what he builds for the next century. The factory floor that feeds everything downstream begins here.

## PART TWO: The Industrial City Rises (1901–1937)

### 1901 — Lansing Car Assembly opens on the Grand River
Ransom Olds builds his car plant on the Grand River. Jobs draw thousands of families to Lansing — many of them Black families moving north for industrial work. A neighborhood grows up in the St. Joseph–Main Street corridor: churches, corner stores, social clubs, the only record store in Lansing that sells rhythm and blues. The city's prosperity and its racial geography are set simultaneously. The neighborhood that will be destroyed in 60 years is being built right now.

*Somewhere in this era, the Norris family is in Lansing. Montie Norris — Jerry's great-grandfather — and his wife Lucy (McEldowney) Norris are building a life in the city. They will have five sons: Ernest, Robert, Keith, Jerry, and John, and daughters. Lucy will live on Olds Avenue for 44 years.*

### 1905 — REO Motor Car Company established
Ransom Olds founds a second company at 1445 S. Washington Avenue after leaving his first. REO manufactures automobiles and trucks until 1975. By 1907, REO has gross sales of $4.5 million and is one of the four wealthiest auto manufacturers in the U.S. The REO Speed Wagon — introduced 1915 — becomes one of the most versatile vehicles in American transportation. The factory floor that will eventually employ Keith Norris is being built.

### 1912 — Michigan Accident Fund created
The state legislature passes the Workmen's Compensation Act (Public Act 10, 1912), creating the Michigan Accident Fund — a public workers' compensation institution, one of the nation's first, and only the second such "Accident Fund" structure in the country after Washington State. Authorized to begin writing coverage Sept. 1, 1912; receives its first policy applications Nov. 25, 1912. A public body, funded as a state agency. It will remain public for 82 years.

*Corrected dating: this entry was previously dated 1908, an error — the Michigan Accident Fund was created by the 1912 Workmen's Compensation Act, confirmed across multiple independent sources including the company's own 100th-anniversary materials.*

### ~1919-1922 — Ottawa Street Station built
Michigan Power Company builds the first Ottawa Street power station on the Grand River. The city acquires it in 1919 after WWI financial strain.

### 1912 — Lansing becomes state capital of a growing industrial state
The city is now home to REO, Oldsmobile, and a growing supplier ecosystem. The factory floor is the center of Lansing's economic and social life.

### 1916 — First boundary expansion
Nearly 60 years after incorporation, Lansing's city limits expand beyond the original seven square miles. The city grows; no new governing nodes are created inside the expanded boundary.

### 1917–1920 — Keith Norris born into the industrial city
**November 29, 1920** — Richard Keith Norris is born in Lansing, son of Montie and Lucy (McEldowney) Norris. He is born into a city at the peak of its industrial confidence. REO and Oldsmobile are thriving. The UAW does not yet exist. The factory floor has no union protection. His grandfather's generation built this city; his generation will fight for the right to share in what it produces.

### 1922–1923 — Eckert Station (Moores Park Plant) built
The Moores Park Plant opens on the Grand River, adjacent to the Oldsmobile assembly complex. Coal-fired. 97 years of combustion begin. No meaningful environmental accountability to the adjacent neighborhood — the same neighborhood that is home to the families who work the plants. It undergoes six stages of rebuilding. The three iconic 615-foot smokestacks — Wynken, Blynken, and Nod — are not added until 1981.

### 1923 — Thelma Arnold born in Boyne Falls
**September 6, 1923** — Thelma E. Arnold is born in Boyne Falls, Michigan, to Jesse and Maude Arnold. Her family's roots run to Beaver Island — the Irish Gaelic fishing community, George T. Arnold, and the older story underneath it. She will spend her working life at MDOT, retire with her husband to a roadside park bearing their name, and outlive two daughters and a grandson.

### 1927 — Bath School massacre, 10 miles north of Lansing
May 18, 1927: Bath Township school treasurer Andrew Kehoe detonates explosives planted under Bath Consolidated School, killing 38 students and adults. The deadliest school massacre in United States history — a record that still stands nearly 100 years later. The injured are brought to Lansing hospitals. A Lansing architect donates the plans for the replacement school. Michigan's history of school violence begins here, 51 years before Kevin Jones's neighborhood.

### 1929–1935 — The Depression hits the factory floor
The Great Depression collapses car sales. GM slashes jobs with no regard for seniority. The average auto worker earns $900 a year against a government-determined minimum of $1,600 for a family of four. GM spends $839,000 on detective work in 1934 alone to identify and intimidate union organizers. Workers are afraid to speak up. The conditions for the strike are being built.

Keith Norris is a teenager during the worst of the Depression, watching his city bleed. He graduates from Lansing Central High School — likely 1938 or 1939.

### 1931 — Boji Tower (Olds Tower) built
The skyscraper at the corner of Michigan and Washington — formerly the Olds Tower — rises as the tallest building in the city. Art Deco symbol of industrial prosperity, built at the peak of the factory era's confidence. The Norris family is somewhere in its shadow.

### 1934 — Kerns Hotel fire
December 11, 1934: fire kills 34 people in the Kerns Hotel, including seven Michigan state legislators. The worst fire disaster in Lansing history. It produces genuine public safety reform — new building codes, new fire safety regulations. The city can learn from disaster when the will exists. The Everett shooting, 44 years later, will prove that will is not always present.

### 1937 — The REO Sit-Down Strike and the Lansing Labor Holiday

**March–April 1937: The REO Sit-Down Strike**
Workers at REO Motor Car Company hold a month-long sit-down strike. Under the leadership of Lester Washburn, UAW Amalgamated Local 182 is established, bringing the UAW to the Lansing area and representing roughly 15,000 automotive workers at REO, Olds, Fisher Body, and other workplaces.

Keith Norris is 17 years old. Still in high school at Lansing Central. But this is his city, and these are the men whose union will eventually protect his wages, his benefits, and his right to speak on the shop floor. The shop floor is learning to speak for itself.

**June 7, 1937: The Lansing Labor Holiday**
At 2:00 a.m., Ingham County Sheriff Allan McDonald cuts the phone lines to the home of UAW Local 182 president Lester Washburn and attempts to arrest him. Finding only his wife **Neva Washburn** home, the sheriff arrests her and leaves three children unattended. He goes home thinking he has broken the organizing drive.

He has done the opposite.

Lester Washburn returns home, calls a meeting at the union hall, and the union votes to call a Labor Holiday — a general strike. The next morning, 12,000 workers, families, and supporters pour into downtown Lansing. They block Capitol Avenue, Michigan Avenue, and Washington Avenue with automobiles and 18-wheelers, parked every which way. They surround the Capitol. They crowd City Hall. Flying squadrons of union members arrive from out of town.

Governor Frank Murphy — a liberal Democrat, friend of labor — arrives by car from Detroit. At 2:30 p.m. he addresses the crowd and promises to broker an agreement. Neva Washburn and the other prisoners are released. Before long, Capital City Wrecking Company negotiates a contract with its workers.

The whole city shuts down for one day. One day. Because a sheriff arrested a woman in the middle of the night and left her children alone. And it worked.

There is now a Michigan Registered Historic Site marker at the intersection of Capitol and Michigan Avenue. A historic marker on a corner most people walk past without knowing what happened there in the summer of 1937, when the shop floor showed what it could do when it acted together.

*"The shop floor knows more about solving problems than the officers in the offices do." — Jerry Norris, ISO auditor, age 23, learning what his grandfather's generation already knew.*

### 1938 — WPA workers build the Lansing City Market
Federal Works Progress Administration workers — government labor during the Depression — build the Lansing City Market on the Grand River off Cedar Street. A genuine public food commons. Built by public hands. Owned by the public. No cooperative structure, no community land trust, no protection for its commons function against future disposal. Eighty-one years from now it will be a shuffleboard bar.

### 1939–1940 — Ottawa Street Power Station completed
The Art Deco Ottawa Street Power Station opens. Designed by Edwyn Bowd. Polychromatic brick, 176 feet over the Grand River, a step-back ziggurat form with a color gradient from black granite at the base to yellow at the top — representing the stages of coal combustion. One of the most beautiful industrial buildings in Michigan. Publicly owned. No resident seat on its governance board. It will be publicly owned for 52 years and then sold.

## PART THREE: War, Return, and the Building Years (1940–1954)

### 1940–1945 — Keith Norris at war
Keith enlists. He participates in the **Normandy Invasion on D-Day** — June 6, 1944 — and fights in the **Battle of the Bulge**. He is awarded the **Purple Heart**. He is one of thousands of Lansing men who leave the factory city for the European theater and come home changed. He comes home to REO Olds, to Thelma, to a city that is about to enter its industrial peak.

*While Keith is fighting in France, Thelma is in Lansing. She will later join MDOT. They will become the first couple to retire together from that agency.*

### 1946 — Monty Norris born; Norris Grocery opens
**April 8, 1946** — Monty L. Norris is born in Lansing, son of Keith and Thelma, named after his great-grandfather Montie.

The same year, **Robert E. Norris** — Keith's brother, Monty's uncle — opens **Norris Grocery at 1327 Olds Avenue**. The address has hosted a continuous series of neighborhood groceries before the Norrises: Bob's, Zilz & Son, Kitchen, and Craig. 1327 Olds Avenue has been a community food anchor for years. Robert takes it over and makes it a family store. It will run for roughly 35 years, until the highway takes it.

Keith comes home from Normandy and goes to work at REO Olds. His brother runs the grocery. His wife Thelma joins MDOT. The Norris family is building — a job, a store, a neighborhood life on the same street where their mother Lucy has lived for 44 years.

### 1947 — Lucy Norris murdered
*Approximate date — exact year to be confirmed through family research and newspaper archives.*

**Lucy Norris**, 71 years old. Formerly a matron at the Lewis Cass building. A resident of the Olds Avenue address for 44 years. Raped and strangled. Her body is found in a rubbish-strewn backyard at 916 S. Butler Boulevard. The Lansing State Journal runs her photo under the words "Victim of Strangler." A 10-year-old boy discovers the body and takes his father to the scene. Her lower dentures are found broken in the driveway.

She is survived by five sons — Ernest, Robert, Keith, Jerry, and John — and three daughters.

Keith has just come home from Normandy. He buried his brothers in the fields of France. He comes home to find his mother murdered on the street where she has lived for 44 years.

*Keith's career after the war runs in three phases: REO Olds on the factory floor; then his own **Standard Oil gas station** — a small business owner, like his brother Robert with the grocery; then MDOT as Equipment Inspector. The Norris men of that generation build things: a store, a gas station, a union card, a wrestling club. They build what the era allows.*

*Whether her killer was ever caught is unknown. It needs to be researched. But the fact of what happened to her — on that street, in that neighborhood, in that era — belongs in the book. She is the ground the family stood on. She is the reason Monty's obituary said that Lucy Norris instilled in him "the values of kindness, resilience, and love." He carried what happened to her his whole life. So does this book.*

### 1948 — Everett District annexed; Keith joins REO Olds
The Old Everett Area on Lansing's Southside is annexed into the city. The boundary expands; no new governance node is created.

Keith Norris is now working the REO Olds floor, carrying a Purple Heart and a union card, building the middle-class life the union makes possible. Young Monty is learning what work looks like from a father who fought for the right to do it with dignity.

### 1950–1953 — Monty delivers groceries on Olds Avenue
Young Monty Norris — in elementary school, growing up on the Norris family's street — delivers groceries for Uncle Robert's store at 1327 Olds Avenue alongside his friend **Gregory Eaton**. Two boys on a Lansing street, building something small and good. Monty will later manage the state agency that acquires this street. Gregory Eaton will become a significant Lansing civic figure. Both will carry these years with them for the rest of their lives.

### 1954 — Frandor opens
The first shopping center in Lansing and the second in Michigan opens near the Lansing/East Lansing border. Acres of impervious parking. 80% impervious cover in the Montgomery Drain service area begins accumulating. No stormwater accounting is required. The public will pay $50 million to fix this watershed in 2020.

## PART FOUR: The Machine Eats the Neighborhood (1958–1970)

### 1958 — I-496 planned through the Black neighborhood
The city's 1958 Comprehensive Master Plan identifies the route for what becomes I-496 — directly through the St. Joseph–Main Street corridor, the neighborhood that grew up around the auto plants. The same streets where the Norris Grocery stands. The same streets where Lucy Norris lived for 44 years.

The decision is made by state highway planners. The neighborhood in its path has no binding council, no veto, no right of first refusal.

Monty Norris is 12 years old. He delivers groceries on Olds Avenue and doesn't yet know what is coming for his uncle's store, his grandmother's street, his community.

### 1960 — North District annexed; Thelma and Keith both working MDOT
A 4.5-square-mile area of northern Delhi Township votes to join Lansing, driven by the need for Lansing School District access. After annexation, Lansing absorbs responsibility for I-96 construction costs through the area. No neighborhood council created.

Keith and Thelma Norris are both now at MDOT. Keith as Equipment Inspector. Thelma on her way to 25 years of service. They are inside the machine that is about to acquire their family's street.

### 1963 — I-496 construction begins; Monty joins MDOT and protests
Construction begins on I-496 in 1963. The first section opens in December.

Monty Norris — 17 years old, just graduated, entering the workforce — joins MDOT around this time. He knows exactly what is happening. The highway is taking his uncle Robert's grocery store at 1327 Olds Avenue. It is taking the street where his grandmother Lucy lived for 44 years before she was murdered there. It is taking the neighborhood where he delivered groceries with Gregory Eaton as a boy.

**He protests. He refuses to work on I-496 in the beginning.**

He stays at MDOT anyway — because you have to eat, because the benefits are real, because there is no alternative that pays as well, because that is what survival inside a system looks like. But he is not complicit. He is a man doing what he can from where he stands. He will spend his career making the taking hurt less for the people who can't stop it.

*A note on the instruction he later gives his son: Monty himself went to college on a **hockey scholarship at Michigan Tech** — there were no wrestling scholarships at Tech. He coached wrestling and co-founded the Capital City Wrestling Club, but his own sport was hockey. When he tells six-year-old Jerry to wrestle in 1972, he is seeing something in his son, not mirroring his own path.*

### 1963–1970 — The displacement
Over seven years of rolling demolition:

- **840+ homes and businesses demolished** via eminent domain
- **600+ families displaced**, nearly all Black
- The only record store in Lansing that sells rhythm and blues — gone
- Churches, social clubs, community centers — gone
- **Norris Grocery, 1327 Olds Avenue** — acquired, demolished, gone
- Lucy Norris's street — gone
- The neighborhood that built itself around the auto plant jobs — gone

What is left: the families south of the new highway, hemmed in on four sides — I-496 to the north, the GM plant to one side, the Grand River to another, a railroad spur on the fourth. A boy named Earvin Johnson Jr. grows up in that hemmed-in pocket. He will become Magic Johnson.

The families that disperse: many move to the Southside, the Westside, into redlined housing markets. Families who paid off their mortgages restart them. The generational wealth gap doesn't close. It compounds.

The highway is named the R.E. Olds Freeway. After the man whose plant generated the jobs that built the neighborhood the highway just destroyed. The displacement is named after its cause.

### December 18, 1970 — I-496 fully opens
Seven years after construction began, the final section between Logan Street and US 127 opens. The freeway is complete. The neighborhood is gone. The Norris Grocery is gone. Robert E. Norris still has 12 years to live. He watches his store taken and goes on.

Monty Norris is 24. He has spent seven years inside the agency that did this. He will spend the rest of his career making sure the families the agency displaces get paid as much as the system allows.

## PART FIVE: Growing Up in What Was Left (1966–1984)

### ~1966 — Jerry Norris born on Summerset
**Jerry Norris** is born in Lansing, the first child of **Monty Norris** and **Margot (DeWeese) Norris**. The family lives on Summerset. The highway that will take his family's grocery store is already under construction. The neighborhood it destroys is already partially gone. Jerry is born into the aftermath.

He carries, without knowing it: the Dutch Quaker blood of Valley Forge, the Irish Gaelic fishing heritage of Beaver Island, the possible Lenape and Odawa ancestry from two different family lines, the REO floor, the Purple Heart, the murdered great-grandmother on Olds Avenue, the grocery store that the highway ate.

### 1969, May 15 — The lawn mower accident
At Keith and Thelma's house on Sommerset, during his little brother's first birthday party, not-quite-three-year-old Jerry sticks his foot in his grandfather's running riding mower. Rushed to Ingham Medical — the hospital he was born in. His foot is nearly severed; doctors doubt he'll walk normally again. He recovers fully. Decades later, in the last conversation before Keith's death — by then deep in Alzheimer's — Jerry tells him it wasn't his fault. Keith, staring ahead, says he remembers doing it on purpose, then walks away.

### 1969–1971 — Divorce; family moves to Armstrong Street
The family leaves Summerset. Jerry's parents divorce — not a surprise, except in the context of a three-year-old, where most things are a surprise. Jerry's mother moves the boys to a duplex on Armstrong Street; his father moves to the Co-op Town Houses on Hughes Road and soon remarries **Joina Combs**, who becomes a significant, steady parent to Jerry and his brother.

### 1971 — Family moves to Julia Street
The family moves to Julia Street. Jerry is approximately 5. The house is 30 feet from Kendon Elementary School. The Jones family lives between the Norrises and the Kendon playground — **Kevin Jones** is Jerry's neighbor.

The neighborhood on Julia Street is working class, tight, built around the schools and the auto plants. The displacement from I-496 is recent history. The families who couldn't get out are now hemmed in. The families who dispersed are building new lives in places like Julia Street.

### 1972 — Monty gives Jerry the instruction
Monty Norris looks at his 6-year-old son and says: **"Wrestle, get a scholarship, and study computer science."**

It is 1972. The personal computer does not yet exist as a consumer product. The internet is a Defense Department experiment. Monty has watched the REO floor and the MDOT office and the eminent domain files long enough to know that the next economy will not be built on factory work. He tells his son where to go before the path exists.

Jerry will follow this instruction exactly. Twelve years later.

### 1972–1976 — Kendon Elementary
Jerry attends Kendon Elementary — the school 30 feet from his house. Kevin Jones attends Kendon too. At every school Jerry attends, he will be sent to a higher-level institution for math classes. The container is never adequate to the ability.

*"Genius is lost in poverty." — Jerry Norris. He is not the smartest kid in his school. He is the one who gets to go to the University of Michigan. The difference is not talent. It is the specific combination of a father's instruction, a coach's advocacy, a developer's apartment, and his own ferocious discipline.*

### 1972–1976 — The busing political battle
The Lansing School Board voluntarily adopts a desegregation plan in 1972. Right away there is resistance. Five board members — including **Hortense Canady**, the first African American elected to the Lansing School Board — are recalled by voters because they supported busing for desegregation. The new board tries to reverse the policy. The NAACP files suit in 1972 — *NAACP v. Lansing Board of Education*. The federal court issues a preliminary injunction in August 1973 restraining the board from reversing the plan.

1974: *Milliken v. Bradley*, the Supreme Court decision that protects Detroit's suburbs from cross-district desegregation orders, ensures that Lansing's suburban ring — DeWitt, Okemos, East Lansing — will never be required to participate. White flight remains a legally protected escape hatch.

1975: A federal judge orders Lansing Public Schools to submit a plan to eliminate "a purposeful pattern of racial segregation." Chief Judge Fox issues the full ruling in May 1976.

### September 1976 — Jerry bused to Vivian Riddle Elementary
Under the court-ordered desegregation program, Jerry Norris — age 10, fifth grade — is bused approximately five miles from Julia Street to **Vivian Riddle Elementary**. He leaves the school 30 feet from his house and rides a bus to a school on the other side of the city. This is the policy designed to fix what I-496 and redlining created. It is imperfect. It is necessary. It is what the system can offer instead of the structural changes that would have made it unnecessary.

### 1978 — Expelled from Riddle
Two gangs are planning a riot at Vivian Riddle. Jerry Norris, 11 years old, reads the room correctly and brings a knife to school for protection. The adults running the school did not prevent the threat. Jerry responds to the threat the only way available to him. He is expelled.

The family moves immediately to **Haag Road** — Section 8 townhouse. Jerry transfers to **Atwood Elementary**, the neighborhood school for the new address.

*A kid expelled for protecting himself in a situation adults created and didn't manage. A family moved to Section 8 housing. Not a failure of character. A failure of the systems that were supposed to protect both.*

### February 22, 1978 — Kevin Jones shot at Everett High School
Jerry's neighbor from Julia Street — **Kevin Jones**, 16 years old — is shot in a hallway at Everett High School after the final bell. His classmate **Bill Draher**, 15, is shot twice and dies. The shooter, **Roger Needham**, 15, hands a gun, a knife, and a box of ammunition to a nearby teacher after the shooting. Police find Nazi literature and a diary called "My Struggle" in his bedroom.

This is **Michigan's first documented student-perpetrated school shooting**.

Kevin Jones survives. He returns to school the following week. Administrators tell his mother it might be better if he looked into alternate education. Kevin and his mother leave. He never sets foot in a classroom again.

The school resumes classes the next day. No active shooter protocol. No structured trauma support. No institutional processing of what happened. A bullet hole remains above locker 02-069 at Everett High.

Kevin Jones develops juvenile diabetes, loses a leg, nearly goes blind. He dies on May 25, 1999, at age 37 — one month and five days after Columbine.

Jerry Norris grows up knowing Kevin Jones. He attends Everett High School himself, years later. He walks past that school knowing what happened there. The violence that took his neighbor's future was not contained or processed — it was papered over.

### 1978–1980 — Atwood Elementary; Gardner Junior High begins
Jerry attends Atwood Elementary on Haag Road. Then moves to **Gardner Junior High**, grades 7 through 9. Three years of middle school. Wrestling begins to take shape. At every school, Jerry is sent elsewhere for math.

### 1979 — Magic Johnson leads the Spartans; Jerry is behind the bench

Margot (DeWeese) Norris — Jerry's mother — is dating a bookie who follows the **1979 MSU Spartans**. This is the team that will win the NCAA Championship, led by sophomore guard **Earvin "Magic" Johnson** — the same Magic Johnson who grew up in the hemmed-in neighborhood south of I-496.

The bookie travels with the team. The team comes to dinner at the house. Players buy cannabis from Margot.

Jerry and his brother Tom are snuck through the locker room before home games. They kneel behind the bench. Magic Johnson is six feet away.

*Jerry is 13 years old, growing up in Section 8 housing on Haag Road, and he is behind the bench of one of the greatest college basketball teams in history. That's the Forrest Gump quality of this life. But it's not magic — it's his mother, surviving and connecting in the ways available to her, giving her sons access to something extraordinary in the middle of an ordinary hard life.*

Jerry Norris graduates from Lansing Eastern High School — the building on Pennsylvania Avenue, one block north of Michigan Avenue, the original 1928 structure. ## PART SEVEN: The Gulf, the Loss, and the Return (2000–2017)

### ~2000 — Jerry begins commuting to Dubai
Jerry Norris begins a 12-year commute to the Gulf. He will spend the equivalent of three years on the ground in **Dubai, Doha, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and India**. He is hired to advise governments on **how to protect the public, including workers**.

He leaves Lansing regularly — for weeks, for months — and returns to find it changing. The GM plant still open. Then closing. The City Market still open. Then closing. The riverfront being rebranded. The housing commission beginning to wind down its stock.

From 6,000 miles away, he watches the arc complete itself.

*What Dubai shows him: what a government builds when it decides to. The scale of deliberate public infrastructure investment. The workers who build it and what their lives actually look like underneath the gleaming surface. The gap between the skyline and the labor camp. And the question that forms over 12 years and three continents: why can't a democracy — with its elections, its accountability mechanisms, its stated commitment to the people — do this better than an authoritarian government that at least has a plan?*

*The answer he arrives at: it can. But not with monocentric governance. Not with a system that concentrates decisions in one center of power, funded by the interests that benefit from those decisions. It needs something else. He doesn't have the name yet. He's getting closer.*

### 2005, May 6 — GM closes Lansing Car Assembly
The longest-operating automobile factory in the United States closes. 104 years from 1901. Jerry's uncle **Chuck Norris** is on the floor. The UAW local that Lester Washburn built in 1937 has represented these workers for 68 years.

Jerry is in Dubai when it happens, or returning, or watching from across the world. Someone calls. What do you say?

### 2006, February 27 — Grandfather Keith dies
**Richard Keith Norris** dies at age 85. D-Day veteran. Purple Heart. REO Olds. Standard Gas Station. MDOT Equipment Inspector. First couple to retire from MDOT with Thelma. Alzheimer's in the end.

He came home from Normandy to a city that was beginning to dismantle the neighborhood his family lived in. He worked for the agency that did it. The public Accident Fund institution — now privatized AF Group — purchases the dormant Ottawa Street Power Station and begins converting it to headquarters. An iconic smokestack is demolished. ### 2009 — The framework clicks: Ostrom, Mondragon, polycentric governance
At some point in the Dubai years — the exact moment is Jerry's to place — the intellectual framework clicks. Elinor Ostrom and the Bloomington School. The Mondragon cooperative model. ICA cooperative principles. Community wealth building. The Cleveland and Preston models.

The thing Jerry has been watching his whole life — the machine that ate the grocery store, the highway, the housing commission, the Chamber PAC, the dual signatures, the purchase options written into leases — has a name. And the alternative has a name too. **Polycentric governance.** Multiple centers of power, cooperative ownership, binding community authority, decisions made by the people who live with their consequences.

His grandfather knew it on the REO floor. Lester Washburn knew it when he shut down the city. The ISO auditor certification told him the shop floor knows more than the officers in the offices. Now he has the vocabulary for the whole architecture.

He knows what he's going to build.

The union Lester Washburn built in 1937 — through a sit-down strike at REO, through the Lansing Labor Holiday, through 12,000 people blocking the streets of downtown with 18-wheelers — is legally weakened in Lansing in 2012. The shop floor that shut down a city cannot stop a lame-duck legislature.

The building where the governor signs the bill is the same Capitol that 12,000 people surrounded in 1937. 75 years. One lifetime.

## PART EIGHT: 1300 Eureka Street (2017–Present)

### March 2014 — The Fledge begins in Grand Ledge
Not after Daniella. Before Daniella. The loss will come to The Fledge three years from now. The Fledge is not built from grief — it is built from knowledge.

Early meetings at **The Log Jam** restaurant in Grand Ledge — coding club for kids and adults. LLC filed with LARA March 2015.

Three models inform it:

**The Cascade Engineering model** — the incubator, the welfare-to-work programs, what you can build with people the system wrote off.

**The Yoor Mom Skateboards model** — a father and a ten-year-old, a city saying no, building it anyway, naming it something that makes you laugh. Joy as resistance.

**The opposite of ISO — chaos.** ISO 9000 is control, standardization, elimination of variance. Jerry was one of the youngest certified auditors in the world. The Fledge is the anti-ISO. Deliberately.

## LINE 5 — KEY TIMELINE ENTRIES

### 1953 — Line 5 built through the Straits of Mackinac — without Tribal consultation
Enbridge (then Lakehead Pipeline Company) receives a 1953 easement from the Michigan Conservation Commission — granted without Tribal consultation and without public input — to lay two 20-inch oil pipelines on the bottom of the Straits of Mackinac. The Straits are where Lake Michigan and Lake Huron connect. They are where the Bay Mills Indian Community's creation story begins. They are sacred Anishinaabe water.

The easement requires anchoring every 75 feet. By 1963, Enbridge already has 17 unsupported spans larger than 75 feet. No action taken. The first compliance violation is documented ten years after construction and is never remediated.

### 2010 — Enbridge Kalamazoo River spill — nation's second-largest inland oil disaster
Enbridge pipeline 6B ruptures near Marshall, Michigan. Over a million gallons of tar sands oil spill into the Kalamazoo River. Cleanup takes years. The company responsible for the worst inland oil spill in US history at that time continues operating Line 5 under the Great Lakes.

### 2018 — Anchor strike on Line 5; three large gouges; no shutdown
A vessel anchor strikes Line 5 in the Straits. Three large gouges in the pipeline. Enbridge's control systems fail to detect the strike. The pipeline runs for over two weeks with no video evidence of its condition. Enbridge never shuts it down. Additional unsupported spans identified — four exceeding 200 feet, the longest 359 feet, approaching the tensile strength of the steel.

### November 13, 2020 — Whitmer revokes the easement
Whitmer issues Notice of Revocation and Termination of Easement to Enbridge. States Line 5 poses "an unacceptable risk of a catastrophic oil spill in the Great Lakes that could devastate our economy and way of life." Orders operations to cease no later than May 12, 2021.

### May 12, 2021 — Enbridge defies the shutdown order. Pipeline keeps pumping.
An Enbridge executive sends a letter to Whitmer and the DNR saying the company will continue to operate the pipeline while it works on the tunnel project. The pipeline keeps pumping 23 million gallons per day. A Canadian oil company tells the Governor of Michigan no. The state ultimately drops its enforcement case. The pipeline is still running as of July 2026.

### PLEASANT LAKE / GRANDMA BRETT — Family Water History

**Pleasant Lake, Henrietta Township, Jackson County** — 269 acres, maximum depth 52 feet, located between Lansing and Jackson on M-106. Jerry spent summers and weekends here as a child; lived there for a summer. Grandma Brett had a place on the water. Fish species: black crappie, bluegill, largemouth bass, northern pike, yellow perch.

The community was originally called Spring Lake until renamed in 1836. Platted 1868. Now has a post office (ZIP 49272), party store, gas station, Dollar General, inn, golf course, Christ Episcopal Church.

**The deep history of that ground:** In 1859, workers digging along what would become M-106 unearthed Native American bones — buried roadside at Harrington Cemetery (also Southwell Cemetery), the oldest graveyard in the township. The township was organized in 1837 as "West Portage" — named for the Anishinaabe portage routes across that landscape. A French-Canadian trader named Jean Baptiste Berard claimed to have settled in Henrietta Township in 1816 and established a trading post by 1831 — before formal European settlement. Nearby Batteese Lake and Batteese Creek named after him. Pleasant Lake was a fur trade corridor before it was a summer cottage community.

**Watershed position:** Pleasant Lake sits in the upper Grand River watershed — near the headwaters region. Jackson County sits on one of the largest aquifers in North America. The Grand River's headwaters begin from natural springs near the Jackson/Hillsdale county line, flow through Jackson, Ingham (Lansing), Eaton, Clinton, Ionia, Kent, and Ottawa counties, and empty into Lake Michigan at Grand Haven. Lake Michigan connects to Lake Huron at the Straits of Mackinac — where Line 5 runs.

**The full water chain:** Pleasant Lake → upper Grand River watershed → Grand River through Lansing (two blocks from The Fledge) → picks up Red Cedar at downtown Lansing → Grand Ledge → Grand Rapids → Lake Michigan at Grand Haven → Straits of Mackinac → Line 5.

Jerry has Anishinaabe ancestry. The bones workers found in 1859 building past Pleasant Lake are part of his family's story.

### LANSING EMBEDDED WATER SYSTEMS — Full Map

**The Saginaw Formation Aquifer** — Lansing's drinking water comes 100% from groundwater. BWL draws from 125 wells drilled approximately 400 feet into the Saginaw Formation, which underlies much of mid-Michigan. Lansing is one of the largest cities in the country to rely exclusively on groundwater. The aquifer is recharged from local surface sources — streams, lakes, wetlands, permeable soils — meaning surface contamination and groundwater are the same system on a long enough timeline. Assessed as "highly susceptible" to contamination due to known sources in and near BWL's wellhead protection areas. In 2010 USGS tested 20 BWL production wells for 69 organic wastewater compounds; detected compounds in half the sampled wells. BWL's Erickson coal ash ponds leaking heavy metals into groundwater; dozens of private wells within a mile pull from the bedrock aquifer.

**Carrier Creek** — urban stream running through Lansing. One of the flashiest (most volatile flow) gauged tributaries in the entire Grand River watershed. Lowest groundwater loading in the watershed — meaning it is almost entirely stormwater runoff with no groundwater buffering. That is what storm drains and parking lots do to a creek.

**Red Cedar River** — originates at Cedar Lake in Livingston County. Flows 51 miles north then west, entering the Grand River in Lansing. Drains 461 square miles. Runs through MSU campus. Supports 30+ fish species including rare freshwater mussels. Cannot be swum in 70% of the time due to E. coli. The Anishinaabe name and cultural significance documented; MSU stocked steelhead trout in 2013.

**Middle Grand River Watershed** — approximately 258 square miles of Mid-Michigan. Takes in the Red Cedar, Looking Glass River, and Maple River as major tributaries. Lansing sits at the confluence of several of these systems.

**The Grand River / O-wash-ta-nong** — 252 miles, longest river in Michigan. Headwaters near Jackson/Hillsdale county line. Second largest watershed in Michigan, comprising 13% of the entire Lake Michigan drainage basin, draining 5,572 square miles across 15 counties. The Anishinaabe called it O-wash-ta-nong — "Far Away Water." An important trade route for centuries before European settlement. Hugh Heward documented a British fur trading trip on the Grand in 1790. Six mainstem dams: North Lansing, Grand Ledge, Portland, Webber, Wagar, and Lyons. Empties into Lake Michigan at Grand Haven.

**Lake Michigan → Straits of Mackinac** — Grand River is 13% of the Lake Michigan drainage basin. Lake Michigan connects to Lake Huron at the Straits — where Line 5 runs 23 million gallons of oil per day. Bay Mills creation story begins at the Straits.

### PROPERTY TAX — STRUCTURAL ANALYSIS (research July 27, 2026)

**Michigan millage system:** Property tax uses mills (1 mill = $1 per $1,000 taxable value). Bills are stacked: city + county + school + state education tax (6 mills statewide) + community college + special millages.

**Lansing city millage — confirmed at statutory maximum:** Michigan Home Rule City Act sets maximum city operating rate at 20 mills. Lansing voters approved 20-mill renewal in 2011, 2016, and 2021 — without the 2021 renewal the rate would have reverted to 18.9740 mills. Lansing has been at the 20-mill statutory maximum for over a decade. Total bill: 53.9054 mills for principal residence (homestead), 71.3532 mills for non-homestead (rental/commercial). Ingham County has the highest effective property tax rate in Michigan at 1.76% — nearly double the national average.

**Proposal A uncapping mechanism:** Taxable value capped at 5% or CPI (whichever is lower) annually while same owner holds. Uncaps to current State Equalized Value (SEV = half market value) when property transfers. Applies to all property types — rental and owner-occupied both get the cap while held, both uncap at sale.

**The non-homestead gap:** Principal Residence Exemption (PRE) removes 18 mills of school operating tax. Renters' landlords don't get the PRE — that 17-18 mill gap is embedded in rent. Tenants have no cap protection; they absorb landlord's full tax bill through rent.

**Lansing 2024 data:** Total SEV $3.719 billion vs. taxable value $2.916 billion — approximately $800 million shielded from taxation by long-term ownership caps. County SEV rose 9.58% from 2023 to 2024. CPI cap hit maximum (5%) in 2022, 2023, and 2024 three consecutive years. Median sale price hit $227,500 April 2024, up 19% year-over-year. Average price $257,888 in 2024, up 15.2%.

**Rental percentage:** 43.6% of Lansing housing units renter-occupied (2022). Michigan statewide: ~27%. Racial breakdown: 62.3% of Black Lansing residents rent vs. 36.1% of white residents. 40% of Lansing rental stock is pre-1980 construction.

**Housing turnover 2020-2024:** Approximately 29,000 metro area sales over five years; estimated 7,000-8,000 city of Lansing sales = approximately 15% of all 51,129 housing units changed hands. Rental properties turn over 2-3x more frequently than owner-occupied, meaning approximately 60-65% of uncapping events hit rental properties. Each uncapping in a hot market triggers immediate full-rate tax bill, which passes to tenant through rent.

**The mechanism (ALICE connection):** Long-term landlords hold at capped taxable values well below market — low tax bills relative to actual equity. New landlords and new buyers uncap immediately at full market value at 71.3 mills. Tenants fund landlord's entire tax bill through rent with no cap protection. Lansing is structurally dependent on housing turnover for tax base growth — and housing turnover in a rental-heavy market means rent increases, and rent increases at the margin mean evictions, and evictions mean unhoused. 49% of Michigan renter households spend more than 30% of income on housing; one in four spends more than half.
